Compiled 15 November 2026 from the BNJC Claude account and its Google Drive. Everything below the "Sources" divider is copied verbatim from the original files; only this top section is new.

## What this project is

- **How to Jew** is Yosef Silver's book project.
- It began as an internal BNJC handbook for colleagues, whatever their Jewish background. Its message: all Jews are welcome and there is no single right way to show up.
- The handbook was printed in September 2026 as a 68-page A5 booklet.
- Yosef is now turning the BNJC version into a publishable book ("a novel of sorts"), written chapter by chapter with Claude, in bursts, over several weeks.
- An earlier, related build called **How Do You Jew** (a web handbook) was lost and restarted on 31 July 2026. Its handoff note is Source 2.

## Standing rules carried over from the sources

These come from the handoff note (Source 2) and were written for the How Do You Jew build. Confirm which still apply to the publishable book.

- Not anti-Orthodox. The book has to make people love Judaism.
- Never invent content. Real examples or none.
- Verify halachic detail, denominational positions and anything near Israel before it ships.
- Register: a funny handbook, not a sitcom. Useful first, wit dry and embedded.
- Every deliverable goes to a repo and to Drive on day one. Nothing lives only in a chat session.
- BNJC house spelling: the restaurant is "Yofi", not "YOFI".

# Chapter One — How to Jew
*Working draft v0.2 — adds the "how this book works" section before the closing invitation; divorce line confirmed staying. Otherwise unchanged from approved v0.1.*

---

The first time — and, so far, the only time — I have ever climbed into a fire engine, it was parked outside my grandmother's art deco flat.

She lived on the sixth floor, with views of the Sussex Downs on one side and the seafront stretching for miles on the other. The wallpaper was gold and velvet stripe, and the flat was a constant of my life until the day she died: always open, whether for a cup of tea, a glass of scotch in front of the evening news, or one of the occasions when trestle tables filled the room and the room somehow agreed to hold them.

So there was nothing strange about the fact that on this particular night, fifty or so of us — aunts, uncles, cousins, friends, neighbours from the building, many of them Jewish too — were squeezed around her table at the end of Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, for the meal that breaks the fast. There was also nothing strange, at least to us, about the fact that only a handful of the fifty had actually fasted, and fewer still had been anywhere near a synagogue. The meal was not a reward for observance. The meal was the observance. Nobody at that table would have put it that way, and nobody needed to.

That was when the fire alarm went off.

It became clear quite quickly that this was not a drill. Fifty of us left the table and filed down the stairs — hundreds of them, it felt like, six storeys' worth — and out into a night already washed in red flashing lights.

My mother and my uncle, without discussion, went back in. They took it upon themselves to go door to door through the building, helping the elderly and the infirm down those same stairs. I should mention that my mother had, at this point, not eaten or drunk anything for twenty-five hours. I was upset about this at the time — not the fasting, the going back in — and registered my protest in the way children do, which is to say I was ignored. My cousins and I were left in the care of the firemen managing the scene, one of whom lifted me into the cab of the engine, which is how I came to make the climb I have never repeated since.

My grandmother, for her part, also had priorities. Before evacuating, she went to the safe and collected her jewellery. Then she put on a fur coat, to keep warm, and came down all six floors dressed for the occasion.

The fire, it turned out, never spread beyond a single flat. Hours later we climbed back up, took our places, and resumed. My mother, who had spent the evening of the holiest day of the year carrying her neighbours' arms down a stairwell, finally broke her twenty-five-hour fast on an apple.

---

That was my grandmother's flat, and this is my family.

My grandmother described herself, without any sense of contradiction, as a Jewish atheist. She helped fundraise and establish the Reform synagogue in Brighton — and then left the congregation because it wasn't religious enough. If that sentence doesn't make sense to you, good. You are exactly the reader I wrote this book for, and by the end of it, it will make perfect sense, and you will have met a hundred people like her.

We were, in those years, chicken soup on Friday night Jews. Roast chicken and potatoes Jews. Chopped liver Jews. My great-grandmother's chicken soup recipe is still made in our family's homes, generations on, which as far as I can tell makes it the most successfully transmitted piece of Jewish law in our entire lineage.

My parents divorced when I was five or six, and — in the way these things sometimes go — both became far more observant afterwards. I spent my formative years in an ultra-Orthodox community in Manchester, black hat and side curls, being taught a Judaism that you did because you did it, not because you understood it. When my teenage rebellion arrived, it took the form you would least expect: I became a modern Orthodox Jew. I kept the faith, kept kosher, kept Shabbat — and, gasp, watched films and listened to the radio. It was not until my sixteenth birthday that a television entered my house, and only then because my grandmother had inspected the situation and formally ruled me a deprived child.

Manchester was so Jewish that I did not make my first non-Jewish friend until after I had left — working in a call centre in Jerusalem. I want you to sit with the geography of that sentence for a moment. I had to move to the Middle East to meet a gentile.

Fast forward to my forties: a divorced father of three, carrying a hundred and one questions about faith and practice and sexuality and prayer and tradition, and none of the answers I had been raised on. I had the enormous luck of finding a rabbi who got all of it — who could tell the difference between the "sacred" and the actually sacred, who approached religion with logic and without dismissing science or history, and who, not to overstate it, saved my relationship with our faith.

Like the generations before me, I have spent my life inside Jewish communities — sometimes as part of their infrastructure, sometimes just by opening my own front door and building the village I wanted around me. And along the way I have learned something from every flavour of Jew there is.

---

Here is what all of it taught me, and here is the premise of this book.

There is no one way to Jew. My mother's way, that night, was the stairwell. My grandmother's way was the fur coat — and she would want you to know the fur coat was also correct. The black hats of Manchester have their way; the Reform congregation my grandmother founded and then flounced out of has theirs; and none of them is the fake version of somebody else's real thing. Nobody is doing this wrong on purpose. Judaism has been evolving for three thousand years, and every Jew you will ever meet is standing somewhere on that moving map, doing their honest best with the coordinates they were given.

And I know what it is like to stand outside the glass, because I once stood on the other side of it. I once knew as little about you as you may know about us. So let me say the thing this whole book exists to say: you don't need to be afraid to be yourself around us. You are allowed to ask. If you are worried a question might be offensive, the worry itself is usually proof that it's fine. We are people — with our quirks, our arguments, our recipes that outlive our beliefs — and most of us, honestly, are quite a nice bunch anyway.

---

A quick word about how this book works, and then we'll get going.

The Jewish year does not run on your calendar. It runs on the moon, which is why the holidays seem to wander around your scheduling app like they've lost their keys — so rather than marching from January to December, we'll walk the year the way we actually live it, starting in September, where it begins. If you only need to know why half your office is out in October, there's a cheat sheet for that; the rest of you can take the scenic route with me.

Between the parts of this book you'll find pages of rapid-fire questions — the ones people are usually too embarrassed to ask out loud, answered the way I'd answer a text from a friend. Can you invite us to your Christmas party? (Yes. We might even come.) Do all rabbis have beards? (No. Stay tuned.) Nothing asked in good faith is off the table; that is roughly the founding principle of my entire life.

And in the practical chapters — food, Shabbat, weddings, mourning — I'll finish with the part you can act on: what to bring, what to say, what never to say in a house of mourning. Whoever you are — colleague, friend, neighbour, in-law-to-be, or a Jew reading over someone's shoulder to check whether I got it right — you're who I wrote this for.

How you Jew is yours. How you come to know us — that part, I can help with. Come on up. The kettle's on, the scotch is open, and if the fire alarm goes off, you'll want to see what my grandmother wears down the stairs.

---

# Chapter Two — What Exactly Are You?
*Working draft v0.3 — spiral rewritten to Yosef's spec; Malaysia/Polanski beats added with legally-safe verified wording; three-reader sidebar removed from this chapter, best line folded into prose. Raw for tearing apart.*

---

The morning after a tornado swept through my neighbourhood in suburban Kansas, I stepped onto the patio and found a sixty-foot tree lying at my feet. Some jobs are not DIY jobs. I hired a professional.

He showed up a few days later — early forties, like me, built solid from a career outdoors, an American flag tattooed on his forearm, the kind of man who shakes your hand and means it. Over the three days he worked in my garden I learned that he volunteered at his church; that he had adopted his special-needs niece when his sister could no longer care for her; that he loved his work because it let him help people on the worst day of their week. By any honest measure, he was an all-American mensch. You'll learn that word later in this book. He earned it.

It is very obvious that mine is a Jewish home. I wear a kippah — I always have, wherever I've been on the winding road of my own observance. There are mezuzot on every doorpost, Israeli radio on the smart speaker, and a name on the invoice, Yosef Silver, that is a giveaway to anyone not trying to sneak an extra "u" between the "o" and the "s."

And he was curious — warmly, unashamedly curious, which as you'll discover is my favourite quality in a person. He asked about the little hat on my head, and whether all Jewish people wear them all the time. He asked about the stream of friends and kids and neighbours who kept nipping in and out of the house unannounced while he worked. He overheard phone calls about Shabbat potlucks and asked about those too. I answered everything, happily. He told me about the richness his church gave his life, and how good it was to see people who actually knew their neighbours' names. For three days we schlepped branches to the woodchipper together, me with my tea, him with his Big Gulp. Then the work was done.

We stood by his truck. He shook my hand and said he'd come back any time I needed. We said we should grab a beer. And then, with the same warmth he'd carried all three days, with the easy smile of a man who had decided he liked me, he said:

"I don't know why guys don't like you Jews. You look white, anyway."

I didn't say anything. I just stood there while it washed over me, and he got in his truck and drove away.

We never got that beer.

---

I want to be fair to him, because he was, in every other moment, a good man — and because I suspect he has no idea that sentence was anything other than a compliment. He meant it as acceptance. And the reason a kind man can say something like that is not malice. It's that he was working from the wrong map.

Here is the map he had, because it's the map almost everyone has. There are three files a person can go in: *religion* — a thing you believe; *ethnicity* — a culture you come from; *race* — a thing you are. Pick one. He'd spent three days trying to work out which file Jews belong in. The potlucks said religion. The food and the open door said culture. And the last sentence, the one he didn't know he was answering, said race.

Let me save you the three days. The answer is yes.

To show you what I mean, I have to tell you where I come from — and then about the worst Sunday morning of my teenage years.

---

From a young age, I was told the tale: my family fled the Spanish Inquisition, made it to Holland, and from there entered England in the era of Cromwell, when the country let the Jews back in after nearly four hundred years of official absence. And for all the generations since, we have been thoroughly, cartoonishly English — the cups of tea, the strong opinions about whether cream or jam goes on the scone first. But I was raised knowing the distinction cold: I am English because of where I am. What I *am* travelled here from somewhere else.

Years later — before I understood how unwise it is to hand your genetic code to a company that might someday sell it — I did one of those at-home DNA tests, curious to see whether the Cromwellian family legend held up. The results came back and settled the question with almost comic finality: to within a rounding error, the test said one thing. Jewish, with the remainder pointing at the hills around the Levant. Three and a half centuries in England, and England had contributed the accent, the tea, and nothing you could find in a cheek swab. Whatever my family had been doing since the Inquisition, marrying out was not it. My Jewish identity, it turns out, was nature all the way down — and then nurture built on top of it.

That's the "race" answer, and I give it to you without flinching, because history has never flinched about it either. When the world has come for us — Spain in the 1490s, Germany in the 1940s — it never once asked what we believed. The Inquisition burned converts to Christianity on suspicion of secret Jewishness; the Nuremberg laws counted grandparents, not prayers. The men with the clipboards always knew what the census forms still can't decide: this thing is in the blood, whether or not it's in the heart.

But blood is only half of what I mean when I say there's no way out. For the other half, I have to take you to Sunday Club.

---

As a teenager, I went to a youth group on Sunday mornings — aptly named Sunday Club — that existed to be a safe room for the conversations the Orthodox world usually files under *taboo*. What would happen if Israel ceased to exist. What Judaism actually says about sex before marriage. Nothing was off the table, which is precisely why we came. I was, at the time, the kind of teenager who kept two kippahs — one for yeshiva, and a different one for being out in the world — which tells you most of what you need to know about being sixteen inside a community that is watching.

One Sunday, our youth leader — today a well-known rabbi and author — posed this: *What would you do if you found out tomorrow morning that there'd been a mistake, and you weren't actually Jewish after all?*

The answers went around the room. Some said they'd convert, obviously — straight to the rabbinical court, restore the paperwork. Others said they'd convert *after* a cheeseburger, on the once-in-a-lifetime technicality. Everyone laughed.

And I sat there spiralling like Truman when he sails his boat into the painted sky, because I couldn't find the version of me that survived the question. Take away *Jewish*, and what happened to Shabbat — the day that stitched my week back together, the table, the songs, the long unhurried walk? What happened to the connection — the people who knew me without explanation, the homes I could walk into unannounced, the certainty that in any city on earth there was a door that would open for me? Even the way I saw the world — the passion for what was right and the fury at what wasn't — I had learned that somewhere, and the somewhere was this. Everything I'd been taught from my youngest days, everything I have questioned since — and I have questioned all of it, daily, up to and including the morning I write these words — where would the questions even live, if the thing I was questioning was never mine? Because that's what I understood, sitting in that room: questioning it was never the same as leaving it. The arguing was the belonging. It wasn't like being asked to change religions. It was like being told the set wasn't real.

That spiral is the honest answer to what Judaism is. Because yes — it is prayer and practice. Rituals and recipes. But underneath all of it, it's a people you belong to, with everything belonging brings: the network that has caught me in every city I've ever lived in, and the prying eyes that made a teenager keep a second kippah in his pocket. Both. The community that will drive you mad is the same one that will show up, uninvited and fully catered, on the worst day of your life. You don't believe your way into that, and you can't disbelieve your way out of it.

And Jewish law, for once, agrees with the lived experience completely: you cannot resign. Stop practicing — still a Jew. Marry out — still a Jew. Convert to something else entirely, announce it in the newspaper — the tradition will shrug and keep your seat. On this one point, grimly, the rabbis and the persecutors have always held the same position. Nobody gets to leave the tribe. It's not a membership. It's kinship — which, if you happen to be dating one of us, is the most useful thing this book will tell you: you are not taking on someone's beliefs, you are joining someone's people, and the family dinner will always matter more than the theology conversation.

Here is the one feature that breaks every category you'd try to put us in: the door does open — inward. A convert arrives with none of my Levantine cheek-swab and becomes fully, completely, irrevocably a Jew. Not an associate member. Not a Jew-with-an-asterisk. In, forever — no-exit clause included; the door swings one way for everybody. We are, as far as I can tell, the only "race" in human history that issues naturalization papers. King David's own great-grandmother was a convert named Ruth. We put her book in the Bible and read it standing up.

A religion that doesn't require belief — you met my grandmother, the synagogue-founding atheist; a friend of mine once put it perfectly: Christianity is for the followers of Christ, but Judaism is how Jews express who we already are, which is why there's no pope, no leader to follow, and a thousand ways to do it. An ethnicity, in a dozen regional flavours that get their own chapter. And a race with an entrance and no exit. Hold all three at once and you finally have the right map.

---

Now you can hear what the man by the truck actually said. Not *I accept you* — *I've cleared you*. You pass. And passing is not belonging; it's a conditional visa, revocable at a glance — and one my family has held before. We passed in Spain, right up until we didn't. Jews were barred from the clubs and quotas and neighbourhoods of "white" England and America within living memory; the whiteness on offer now is recent, partial, and — every Jew quietly knows — returnable.

I don't even have to reach back for proof. In the very week I write this sentence, the prime minister of Malaysia announced that any Israeli found in his country will be deported on sight. In London, the leader of the Green Party — himself Jewish — has backed a public call for the government to keep records of British nationals who served in Israel's army, with extra screening at the border; its authors insist it has nothing to do with Jews, while the Board of Deputies calls it a way of marking people out for intimidation. Wherever you stand on any of it, notice the shape: lists, borders, papers. The oldest suspicions never die. They just update their vocabulary.

Besides, the premise fails on contact: walk into any synagogue in Jerusalem and try to sort the room by your racial categories. You'll give up by the second row.

He looked at me carefully for three days and decided I was acceptable. What I'd thought we were doing, over the branches and the tea and the Big Gulps, was becoming known.

---

The tree, in the end, became firewood. It burns in my firepit on Saturday nights while we make Havdalah, the little ceremony that ends Shabbat — the tornado that started all of this now keeping my tribe warm in the garden.

He asked every question right, that man, for three days — until the one he didn't know he was asking. This chapter exists so that you never have to answer it the way he did. You now know what we are. All of it. Yes.

---

# Chapter Two — What Exactly Are You?
*Working draft v0.2 — rewrite per Yosef's note on v0.1: the second half now runs on his stories (family lore, DNA test, Sunday Club) instead of taxonomy. Raw for tearing apart.*

---

The morning after a tornado swept through my neighbourhood in suburban Kansas, I stepped onto the patio and found a sixty-foot tree lying at my feet. Some jobs are not DIY jobs. I hired a professional.

He showed up a few days later — early forties, like me, built solid from a career outdoors, an American flag tattooed on his forearm, the kind of man who shakes your hand and means it. Over the three days he worked in my garden I learned that he volunteered at his church; that he had adopted his special-needs niece when his sister could no longer care for her; that he loved his work because it let him help people on the worst day of their week. By any honest measure, he was an all-American mensch. You'll learn that word later in this book. He earned it.

It is very obvious that mine is a Jewish home. I wear a kippah — I always have, wherever I've been on the winding road of my own observance. There are mezuzot on every doorpost, Israeli radio on the smart speaker, and a name on the invoice, Yosef Silver, that is a giveaway to anyone not trying to sneak an extra "u" between the "o" and the "s."

And he was curious — warmly, unashamedly curious, which as you'll discover is my favourite quality in a person. He asked about the little hat on my head, and whether all Jewish people wear them all the time. He asked about the stream of friends and kids and neighbours who kept nipping in and out of the house unannounced while he worked. He overheard phone calls about Shabbat potlucks and asked about those too. I answered everything, happily. He told me about the richness his church gave his life, and how good it was to see people who actually knew their neighbours' names. For three days we schlepped branches to the woodchipper together, me with my tea, him with his Big Gulp. Then the work was done.

We stood by his truck. He shook my hand and said he'd come back any time I needed. We said we should grab a beer. And then, with the same warmth he'd carried all three days, with the easy smile of a man who had decided he liked me, he said:

"I don't know why guys don't like you Jews. You look white, anyway."

I didn't say anything. I just stood there while it washed over me, and he got in his truck and drove away.

We never got that beer.

---

I want to be fair to him, because he was, in every other moment, a good man — and because I suspect he has no idea that sentence was anything other than a compliment. He meant it as acceptance. And the reason a kind man can say something like that is not malice. It's that he was working from the wrong map.

Here is the map he had, because it's the map almost everyone has. There are three files a person can go in: *religion* — a thing you believe; *ethnicity* — a culture you come from; *race* — a thing you are. Pick one. He'd spent three days trying to work out which file Jews belong in. The potlucks said religion. The food and the open door said culture. And the last sentence, the one he didn't know he was answering, said race.

Let me save you the three days. The answer is yes.

To show you what I mean, I have to tell you where I come from — and then about the worst Sunday morning of my teenage years.

---

From a young age, I was told the tale: my family fled the Spanish Inquisition, made it to Holland, and from there entered England in the era of Cromwell, when the country let the Jews back in after nearly four hundred years of official absence. And for all the generations since, we have been thoroughly, cartoonishly English — the cups of tea, the strong opinions about whether cream or jam goes on the scone first. But I was raised knowing the distinction cold: I am English because of where I am. What I *am* travelled here from somewhere else.

Years later — before I understood how unwise it is to hand your genetic code to a company that might someday sell it — I did one of those at-home DNA tests, curious to see whether the Cromwellian family legend held up. The results came back and settled the question with almost comic finality: to within a rounding error, the test said one thing. Jewish, with the remainder pointing at the hills around the Levant. Three and a half centuries in England, and England had contributed the accent, the tea, and nothing you could find in a cheek swab. Whatever my family had been doing since the Inquisition, marrying out was not it. My Jewish identity, it turns out, was nature all the way down — and then nurture built on top of it.

That's the "race" answer, and I give it to you without flinching, because history has never flinched about it either. When the world has come for us — Spain in the 1490s, Germany in the 1940s — it never once asked what we believed. The Inquisition burned converts to Christianity on suspicion of secret Jewishness; the Nuremberg laws counted grandparents, not prayers. The men with the clipboards always knew what the census forms still can't decide: this thing is in the blood, whether or not it's in the heart.

But blood is only half of what I mean when I say there's no way out. For the other half, I have to take you to Sunday Club.

---

As a teenager, I went to a youth group on Sunday mornings — aptly named Sunday Club — that existed to be a safe room for the conversations the Orthodox world usually files under *taboo*. What would happen if Israel ceased to exist. What Judaism actually says about sex before marriage. Nothing was off the table, which is precisely why we came. I was, at the time, the kind of teenager who kept two kippahs — one for yeshiva, and a different one for being out in the world — which tells you most of what you need to know about being sixteen inside a community that is watching.

One Sunday, our youth leader — today a well-known rabbi and author — posed this: *What would you do if you found out tomorrow morning that there'd been a mistake, and you weren't actually Jewish after all?*

The answers went around the room. Some said they'd convert, obviously — straight to the rabbinical court, restore the paperwork. Others said they'd convert *after* a cheeseburger, on the once-in-a-lifetime technicality. Everyone laughed.

And I sat there spiralling like Truman when he sails his boat into the painted sky. Because I couldn't find the version of me that survived the question. My friendships, my family's table, my Sundays, my two kippahs and everything they were negotiating between — if you removed *Jewish* from me, I didn't know what was left holding the rest up. It wasn't like being asked to change religions. It was like being told the set wasn't real.

That spiral is the honest answer to what Judaism is. Because yes — it is prayer and practice. Rituals and recipes. But underneath all of it, it's a people you belong to, with everything belonging brings: the network that has caught me in every city I've ever lived in, and the prying eyes that made a teenager keep a second kippah in his pocket. Both. The community that will drive you mad is the same one that will show up, uninvited and fully catered, on the worst day of your life. You don't believe your way into that, and you can't disbelieve your way out of it.

And Jewish law, for once, agrees with the lived experience completely: you cannot resign. Stop practicing — still a Jew. Marry out — still a Jew. Convert to something else entirely, announce it in the newspaper — the tradition will shrug and keep your seat. On this one point, grimly, the rabbis and the persecutors have always held the same position. Nobody gets to leave the tribe. It's not a membership. It's kinship.

Here is the one feature that breaks every category you'd try to put us in: the door does open — inward. A convert arrives with none of my Levantine cheek-swab and becomes fully, completely, irrevocably a Jew. Not an associate member. Not a Jew-with-an-asterisk. In, forever — no-exit clause included; the door swings one way for everybody. We are, as far as I can tell, the only "race" in human history that issues naturalization papers. King David's own great-grandmother was a convert named Ruth. We put her book in the Bible and read it standing up.

A religion that doesn't require belief — you met my grandmother, the synagogue-founding atheist; a friend of mine once put it perfectly: Christianity is for the followers of Christ, but Judaism is how Jews express who we already are, which is why there's no pope, no leader to follow, and a thousand ways to do it. An ethnicity, in a dozen regional flavours that get their own chapter. And a race with an entrance and no exit. Hold all three at once and you finally have the right map.

---

Now you can hear what the man by the truck actually said. Not *I accept you* — *I've cleared you*. You pass. And passing is not belonging; it's a conditional visa, revocable at a glance — and one my family has held before. We passed in Spain, right up until we didn't. Jews were barred from the clubs and quotas and neighbourhoods of "white" England and America within living memory; the whiteness on offer now is recent, partial, and — every Jew quietly knows — returnable. Besides, the premise fails on contact: walk into any synagogue in Jerusalem and try to sort the room by your racial categories. You'll give up by the second row.

He looked at me carefully for three days and decided I was acceptable. What I'd thought we were doing, over the branches and the tea and the Big Gulps, was becoming known.

**If you're the coworker:** you never need to solve which file your Jewish colleague goes in. "Jewish" is the file. And "is it a religion or an ethnicity?" is a completely fine lunch question — asked with curiosity, it lands as interest, not offence.

**If you're the friend:** don't be confused when your Jewish friend eats bacon, skips synagogue, and still calls themselves deeply Jewish. Nothing is contradictory. Membership was never conditional on practice — remember the room full of teenagers who couldn't imagine themselves out of it.

**If you're the one dating us:** understand early that you're not dating someone's beliefs, you're joining someone's people — which is why the family dinner matters more than the theology conversation, and why "would our kids be Jewish?" has a technical answer that varies by movement and an emotional answer that doesn't.

---

The tree, in the end, became firewood. It burns in my firepit on Saturday nights while we make Havdalah, the little ceremony that ends Shabbat — the tornado that started all of this now keeping my tribe warm in the garden.

He asked every question right, that man, for three days — until the one he didn't know he was asking. This chapter exists so that you never have to answer it the way he did. You now know what we are. All of it. Yes.

I want to be fair to him, because he was, in every other moment, a good man — and because I suspect he has no idea that sentence was anything other than a compliment. He meant it as acceptance. He had looked at me closely for three days, gotten to know me, and cleared me. And the reason a kind man can say something like that is not malice. It's that he was working from the wrong map.

Here is the map he had, because it's the map almost everyone has. There are three files a person can go in: *religion* (a thing you believe), *ethnicity* (a culture you come from), or *race* (a thing you are). Pick one. He'd spent three days trying to work out which file Jews belong in — the church-like potlucks said religion, the food and the in-jokes said culture, and the last sentence, the one he didn't know he was answering, said race.

So let me save you three days. The answer to "which one are you?" is yes.

---

**A religion — but not the kind you're thinking of.**

A dear friend once put it to me like this: Christianity is for the followers of Christ. Judaism is how Jews express who we already are. It's the single most useful sentence in this chapter, so read it twice.

A Christian is defined by belief — accept the creed and you're in; renounce it and you're out. Judaism doesn't work that way, because belief was never the membership card. You've already met my grandmother, the self-described Jewish atheist who helped found a synagogue and then left it for not being religious enough. In most faiths that's a paradox. In ours it's a Tuesday. She wasn't a lapsed Jew or a bad Jew; she was a Jew, expressing it her way, at full volume.

That's also why nobody is following a leader. We revere Moses; we don't worship him — he doesn't even appear in the Passover story as we tell it at the table, and that's deliberate. There's no pope, no central authority, no head office to appeal to. Rabbis are teachers, not intermediaries; the general management style, historically, has been argument. When the traditions are how a people expresses itself rather than a doctrine it complies with, of course the practice comes out in a thousand versions. Cuisines don't have popes either.

**An ethnicity — obviously.**

The food, the languages, the humour, the guilt, the inability to end a phone call in under four attempts. I told you my great-grandmother's chicken soup recipe has outlived every theological position anyone in my family ever held; that is ethnicity doing what ethnicity does. Which ethnicity, though, is a better question than you know — Ashkenazi, Sephardi, Mizrahi, Ethiopian, Yemenite — and it gets its own chapter, because two thousand years of being scattered across the planet turned one people into a family of cultures that argue about rice.

**And a race — with one feature no other race has.**

This is the part people flinch from, so I won't. Yes, a race. Many of us share ancestry you can trace across millennia and continents; it's in the DNA, literally — geneticists can spot my family tree from a cheek swab. And whenever it has mattered most, the world has treated us as exactly that. The Nuremberg laws did not ask about synagogue attendance. Baptism saved nobody. The men with the clipboards counted grandparents, not beliefs.

But here's the thing — and this is where Judaism differs from every other category you'd call a race: there is no exit, and there is an entrance.

No exit: you cannot resign. Stop practicing — still a Jew. Marry out — still a Jew. Convert to something else, announce your departure in the newspaper — Jewish law will shrug and hold your seat. On this one point, grimly, the rabbis and the persecutors have always agreed: nobody gets to leave the tribe. It's not a subscription. It's kinship.

An entrance: and yet you can join. A convert arrives with zero Jewish DNA and becomes fully, completely, irrevocably a Jew — not an associate member, not a Jew-with-an-asterisk. In. Forever. Which, notice, includes the no-exit clause; the door swings one way for everybody. We are, as far as I can tell, the only race in human history that issues naturalization papers. King David's great-grandmother was a convert. We put her book in the Bible.

Hold all three of those at once — a religion without a required belief, an ethnicity in a dozen regional flavours, a race you can join but never leave — and you finally have the right map. You also understand why the census form has never known what to do with us.

---

**About "you look white, anyway."**

Now you can hear what that sentence actually said. Not *I accept you* — *I've cleared you*. You pass. And passing is not belonging; it's a conditional visa, revocable at a glance, and half my family couldn't get one — walk into any synagogue in Jerusalem, or plenty in London, and try to sort the room into your racial categories. You'll give up by the second row. Jews were barred from the clubs, quotas and neighbourhoods of "white" America and England within living memory; the whiteness on offer now is recent, partial, and — every Jew quietly knows — returnable.

He looked at me carefully for three days and decided I was acceptable. What I'd wanted, what I'd thought we had, was to be *known*.

## 6\. Print production

These are properties of the file itself, and they matter more than any of the above if it's going to a commercial printer.

**The PDF was exported from Chrome (Skia/PDF), not from a layout application.** That produces most of what follows.

1. **No bleed.** MediaBox, CropBox, BleedBox, TrimBox and ArtBox are all identical at 420 × 594.96 pt (A5 trim). Every full-bleed element — the coloured section dividers, the blue appendix pages, the cover photo — will show a white sliver wherever the guillotine wanders. You need 3 mm bleed on all four sides and crop marks, i.e. a 426 × 601 pt media box with the trim box set inside it.  
     
2. **Playfair Display is embedded as Type 3 fonts.** Type 3 is a drawing-procedure format, not a standard outline font; many print RIPs handle it badly or reject it, and it can't be hinted or overprinted properly. Lato is fine (CID TrueType). This alone is a good reason to rebuild the file out of InDesign or Affinity rather than the browser.  
     
3. **RGB, no output intent, PDF 1.4 — not PDF/X.** Printers will want PDF/X-1a or X-4 with a CMYK output intent. Left as is, the blues and the deep red section colour will shift on press.  
     
4. **The cover photograph is 192 ppi** (892 × 595 px placed at full width). 300 ppi is the norm; at A5 it will look soft. The logo, at 441 ppi, is fine.  
     
5. **The 🚨 on the emergency panels is Apple Color Emoji**, a colour bitmap font. In CMYK print this is unpredictable — it may drop out, print as a black box, or rasterise coarsely. Replace it with a vector icon in your brand palette.  
     
6. **Straight quotes throughout the body text.** This is the most visible typographic problem in the book. The file contains 330 straight apostrophes against 56 typographic ones, and 84 straight double quotes against 9 curly pairs — the display type got smart quotes, the body text didn't. In Playfair and Lato at print size, straight quotes read as vertical tick marks. A global find-and-replace pass is needed, taking care not to flip the apostrophe in *'no'*, *L'chaim* and similar.  
     
7. **The file is untagged.** Irrelevant for print, but if you also distribute the PDF, it isn't screen-reader accessible and has no document structure.  
     
8. **68 pages divides by 4**, so it works for saddle-stitch or perfect binding without a blank insert. Nothing to do here.

# **How to Jew**

### **The unofficial reference guide**

Every person on this site tells the world what BNJC is, far more than any campaign will. The one who pours the coffee, checks a guest in, or answers the phone is asked, daily, to serve a community whose rules nobody ever explained to them, and right now they either guess or nod along.

This book fixes that.

It answers, warmly and without embarrassment, the questions staff actually field about kosher, Shabbat, the festivals, and the people who come through the door, so that a member is met with understanding rather than a blank look.

The payoff is not only kindness. A person who knows why the oven can't be switched back on, or why the lettuce is checked leaf by leaf, protects the kosher certification the restaurant trades on and enforces it willingly, because they get it. Literacy here is warmth and reliability at once, and both are worth money  
**Author’s note**

I could see the roof of my school from my bedroom window, and I was still late nearly every morning. We lived on Westbourne Villas, close enough that the walk should have been impossible to get wrong, and this plot of land, the one you're standing on, was the whole of my small world. There was a school here then. It was knocked down to build the beautiful thing that surrounds you now, but for a few years in the beginning it was where I learned, more or less, how to be a person.

You came in and the library was on your left. That's where we held morning prayers and where I learned to find my way around a prayer book, which is a genuine skill, a bit like learning to read sheet music, except everyone is singing already and nobody shows you the page. On your right was the Mark Luck Hall, which led a triple life: karate club in the week, the shul hall for kiddush every Saturday, and Sunday club and youth group after that. One room, three completely different sets of people, each certain it was theirs. I have thought about that hall a lot since. It turns out to be a fairly good description of Jewish life in general.

The staircase, which I remember as grand, though I was small and may not be a reliable witness, is where Mrs Efune declined to tie my shoelace. I had asked her to, coming in from break, fully expecting the service. She told me, kindly, that she thought I could manage it myself, and then she waited, did not do it for me, did not hurry me, while I sat and fought with the thing, and when I finally got it she applauded like I'd done something remarkable. Which, to me, I had.

There was hot chocolate from Rabbi Efune in the kitchen the morning it snowed, waiting for us when we came in cold. There were the birthday lunches Mrs Stark made, so that one day a year the food was yours and everyone knew it. And there was the spelling test where a very sharp pencil somehow ended up lodged in my forearm, and Mrs Smith calmly and patiently helped me get it out, just as we were being asked to spell the word "synagogue," which, given what I do for a living now, I have decided to read as a sign.

This plot of land didn't only teach me how to Jew. It taught me how to read two alphabets, how to respect the people around me, and how to sit still and get the shoelace done myself. And then, decades later, having lived in England and Israel and America and picked up rather a lot of opinions along the way, I was asked to come back to the same ground and help tell the world what this place is.

So here's my one qualification, and it's not a grand one. I am not a rabbi, if you need to know whether something is genuinely kosher, this book will point you to the people who actually decide that. What I am is someone this place raised, who left, who saw how Jewish life is done in a dozen different rooms in three different countries, and who came home to find the school of my childhood had become something bigger and more alive than any of us who sat on that staircase could have pictured.

I've been given the honour of running marketing, communications and brand here, which is a large title for a simple idea: everyone on this site is the brand. The person pouring the coffee, checking a guest in, wiping down the gym, answering the phone, they tell the world what this place is, far more than I ever will. And every one of them deserves the chance to actually understand who they're serving, warmly and without embarrassment, instead of nodding along to things nobody ever bothered to explain.

That's the whole job of this book. I'm just paying forward what this ground gave me.  
And maybe, in thirty-five years, one of the children in Shoresh downstairs will have lived all over the world and come home to Brighton, and will sit down to plan their first weeks in some new role on this site, and will need someone to have written all this down. This is for them, too.

Read it in any order. Skip to what you need. Nothing here is off-limits and nothing is a test. 

Come in curious. That's the only thing this place ever asked of me, and I turned out fine, I even learned to tie my shoes.

# **You're allowed to ask questions\!**

Jews are curious by nature. We ask a lot of questions. If you have ever heard of the Talmud, it is literally volumes and volumes of debate on Jewish theory and practice. Some of it gets answered. Some of it just gets written down for the sake of it. And often there isn't one right answer.

So we are going to lean into that. This whole book is an invitation to ask the things you have always wanted to ask and been too worried to say out loud. And if there is something you are curious about that isn't in here, raise your hand and come find me. If I can't answer it, I will find you the person who can.

**Am I allowed to ask questions about Judaism at all?**  
Yes. Please do. You work in a Jewish building, and understanding it beats nodding along to it. The only rule is the obvious one: ask with respect, and read the room. A question that's perfect over a quiet coffee is a bad one to shout across a busy Friday service. Get the moment right and there's almost nothing you can't ask.

**What if I say the wrong word, or get it wrong?**  
You will, and it doesn't matter. Language is hard, Hebrew doubly so, and half these words shift depending on whose grandmother is saying them. Nobody is marking you. A warm, mangled attempt beats a nervous silence every time, and the person you said it to will have forgotten the fumble long before they forget that you tried.

**Is "Jew" a rude word?**  
No. It's just the word, and Jewish people use it about themselves all day. It can sound risky, usually because the last time you heard it out loud it was in the wrong mouth. Said plainly and warmly it's exactly right. "Jewish person" works too if it sits more comfortably. You don't need to lower your voice.

**Is "Zionist" a bad word?**  
No, though the last couple of years have done a number on it. Underneath the noise it means the belief that Jewish people, like most peoples, have a right to a home. It comes in a dozen flavours that argue with each other endlessly, and for most Jews it isn't a hot political stance, it's just there, woven in. You don't need a position on any of it to work here. Just know that when a member calls themselves a Zionist, they're telling you about belonging, not the thing the word has been twisted into online.

**What is October 7, and why does it come up?**  
On 7 October 2023, Hamas attacked Israel. Around 1,200 people were killed and about 250 taken hostage. It was the worst day for Jews since the Holocaust. For most Jewish people it didn't land as distant news, it landed as something that happened to family, because that's how a people who are also a family feel these things. You'll notice the after-effects here without anyone spelling them out. You don't need a view. You just need to know it's recent, it's personal, and a bit of gentleness costs nothing.

**Is there anything I actually shouldn't ask?**  
Barely one thing. Don't audit people. "You drove here on Saturday but you keep kosher?" feels like curiosity and lands like an accusation, because you're grading someone's consistency instead of asking about the thing itself. Everyone here sits somewhere on a long, personal spectrum of observance, and none of them owes you a scorecard. Ask what kosher is and you'll get a warm answer. Ask someone to justify the gap between two of their own choices and you've become their least favourite relative.

**Can I join in? Say Shabbat Shalom back, try the Hebrew, take the food?**  
Yes, all of it, and it lands warmly. Nobody hands you a quiz first. Have a go.

**What is Orthodox and what does it mean in the context of Judasim and my work here?**  
Orthodox means keeping the tradition closely, treating Jewish law as binding and living by it day to day. That's the whole of the definition. Orthodox tradition  is the expression of Judaism that we adhere to as a community center, and it’s important to note that everyone is welcome on site, no matter how they connect with their Judaism.  When it comes to deciding what is kosher or what prayer service we will follow, those decisions will be based in the orthodox tradition.

# **What is Kosher all about?**

Kosher literally means "proper," but for our purposes it's about food, and one question: can someone who follows the laws of kashrut eat it? There's a big difference between what happens to be kosher and what is supervised and endorsed as kosher, and in this building we're talking about the second kind, the endorsement our restaurant carries and has to protect.

That also means people may keep things differently in their own homes, or hold different opinions that differ.  There’s an age-old fable of a Jewish man being rescued from a desert island being asked why he built two synagogues while trying to survive… the one he went to, and the one would never attend\! This section is about the standard of kosher we follow at BNJC, and why.

**Is the food blessed by a rabbi?**  
No. This is the one you'll hear guests explain to each other, wrongly, while you stand there holding the bread. Kosher isn't a blessing. It's a system: rules about ingredients, about keeping certain things apart, and someone on site making sure it all actually happens. The rabbi is the auditor, not a wizard.

**What actually makes meat kosher?**  
Four things, roughly. The animal has to be on the permitted list. It has to be slaughtered by a trained specialist, quickly and by hand. It gets inspected. And the blood is salted out, because blood is never eaten. That's also why kosher meat costs more, and why the "kosher-style" salt beef in an ordinary deli isn't kosher at all. It has the recipe. It doesn't have any of that.

**Are all fish kosher?**  
Fins and scales. That's the whole test. Salmon, tuna, haddock, fine. Prawns, lobster, mussels, squid, never. 

**Why is there no dairy anywhere in the kitchen?**  
Meat and milk are kept completely apart in Jewish law, and the cleanest way to guarantee that in a meat kitchen is to have no dairy at all. None. Not in a sauce, not in the pastry section, not in a fridge for staff coffees. The oat milk in your flat white is there for a reason, and the reason is the whole kitchen.

**Why can’t I make a cappuccino with milk for someone after they had a burger?**  
Because after meat, observant people wait before they have anything with milk in it. How long depends on where their family is from. So the coffee getting turned down is not a verdict on your coffee. Some of them will explain it by saying "I'm still meaty." It's a good phrase. Let them have it.

**Why can we use baby spinach but not full-size spinach?**  
Someone genuinely asked this in the restaurant, and it has a real answer: bugs. Kosher law takes eating even one insect seriously, so leaves have to be checked, and baby leaves are far easier to check than big mature ones that hide more in the folds. It's a fairly modern level of strictness and we follow it because our certification depends on it. Not fussiness. The endorsement.

**What's the deal with checking the lettuce leaf by leaf?**  
Same reason, and here's the fact worth keeping: eating one small insect breaks more separate rules than eating bacon does. So the leaves and the berries and the herbs get gone over properly, on a light box, slowly. The chef inspecting a raspberry like it's done something wrong is doing exactly what the certificate requires.

**Is all vegan food automatically kosher?**  
No. Kosher isn't only about what the food is, it's about how it was made and what it was made on. A tofu dish finished with a non-kosher wine reduction isn't kosher. A latke fried in a pan that had bacon in it that morning isn't kosher. No meat and no milk gets you started and nowhere near finished.

**Why does it matter who turns the oven on?**  
For a lot of observant guests, the cooking counts as kosher when a Jew had a hand in lighting the flame, so the supervisor lights it and everything after is covered. What this means for you: if an oven is switched off, don't switch it on to be helpful. You'd be undoing the whole arrangement without knowing it. Go find the mashgiach.

**Who is the mashgiach, and why do they outrank the chef?**  
The mashgiach is the kashrut supervisor, and they don't work for the restaurant, they work for the people who give us the certificate. On one question, and only one, is this kosher, they beat everybody. The chef, the manager, whoever. On everything else the kitchen runs normally. On that, they decide.

**Is all wine kosher? And is wine holy?**  
No, and not quite. Wine is the odd one out: it isn't kosher unless it was made kosher the whole way through, while most beer and whisky are fine as they are. That's because wine is how Jews mark the moments that matter. Friday night starts over it. Shabbat ends over it. Weddings, festivals, the four cups at Passover. It isn't holy exactly, but it carries the holy, and things that carry the holy pick up rules along the way.

I've got a story about this. I was in Hungary, tasting wine with a friend in an Israeli restaurant, and after we'd tried it we turned the bottle round and there was a picture of Jesus on the label. We'd managed to find, in a kosher restaurant, the one bottle of wine on earth that almost certainly wasn't kosher by anyone's standard. It happens.

**I thought Jews didn't drink?**  
You're thinking of a different faith. There's no rule against it. What there is, is a habit of drinking at celebrations (and one day a year, Purim, where slightly overdoing it is close to the point). The whisky at Saturday morning kiddush does not lack for volunteers.

**A guest asked "can I go wash?" What are they washing?**  
Their hands, ritually, before bread, at the station with the big two-handled cup. Not the toilet. It's a small act of getting ready for the meal rather than anything about hygiene. Point them the right way. Then watch what happens at the table.

**Why did a whole table go silent for thirty seconds?**  
Between washing and the blessing over the bread, you don't talk. So a loud table drops into total silence, running on eyebrows, until someone blesses the bread and the noise comes straight back. Nothing is wrong. Don't go over to check the meal's alright. Bring the starters and let them get to the amen.

**Why is the table mumbling or singing after dessert?**  
Grace after the meal. Sometimes the whole table sings it, sometimes each person murmurs it from a little booklet. It means they're done and the food was good. One thing to know: those booklets have God's name in them, so they never go in the bin. Find strays while you're clearing, stack them, hand them in.

**Can guests bring their own food in?**  
No, and the reason is nicer than the rule sounds. The kosher certificate promises that everything under this roof is kosher, and that promise is exactly why a guest who cross-examines the menu at every other restaurant in Britain can walk in here and just order. One sandwich from the outside could break that entire promise.  Even kosher food from the YOFI Deli can not be consumed in the restaurant as it made be dairy, and the restaurant serves meat. So it's no, said kindly, with an actual reason behind it.

# **On the seventh day he rested…**

Shabbat is everything to me. If you have never experienced a Friday Night dinner, grab your phone right now and ask your favourite Jewish person if you can come for Shabbat some time soon.

For me there is a moment on Friday night where the entire week resets. Everything melts away. The chaos of the work week, the noise of the family, the deadlines, the problems, it all stops. For the strictly observant, Shabbat is a twenty-five hour reprieve from the world, from sundown on Friday to nightfall on Saturday. No phones, no work, no travel, no money, no cooking. It's time for family, and prayer, and community, and quiet. A forced reset.

For others it might be Friday Night dinner with family before heading into town for drinks. Everyone's relationship with religion is their own. But we're going to walk through some of the oddities that come up during a short stay or a Shabbat meal with people keeping it to the letter.

**What actually is Shabbat?**  
A full day of stopping. It's the day of rest, in honour of the day God rested after making the world, and it runs from sundown on Friday to nightfall on Saturday. No work, no phones, no spending, no travel. Food, family and time, all made deliberately slow. Once a week the world is told to wait.

**Hang on, is it shabbat, or shabbos?**  
Both. Same word, two pronunciations, and which one someone uses tells you where they're from more than what they believe. It’s the scone or scone of the day of rest.

**Why don’t people use electricity on Shabbat?**  
Shabbat pauses the acts of making and creating, and completing an electrical circuit gets treated as one of them. So lights, ovens, phones and switches are all set before sundown and left alone until it's over. It looks like a rule about electricity. It's really a rule about not building things on your day off.

**What's with the lift stopping at every floor on a Saturday?**  
That's Shabbat mode. Orthodox Jews observing the sabbath don’t want to press a button, so the lift is set to stop at every floor on its own and you just ride along. It's a piece of engineering built entirely so that a person can have a day off from pressing buttons. Marvel at it briefly, then get on.

**Why does Friday close early, and why does the time of Shabbat keep moving?**  
Because it all runs on the sunset, and the sunset moves. Shabbat starts when the sun goes down on Friday, which in a Brighton December is the middle of the afternoon and in June is the evening. The wall keeps moving through the year. Check the time, don't assume it.

**Why did a guest ask ME to turn on a light or press a button?**  
Because they can't, and you can, and asking is allowed where doing it themselves isn't. It's not cheeky and it's not an order. You are completely free to say yes or to say no. Plenty of people are asked and happily help, and that's the whole of it.

**What is a Shabbos goy, and is it offensive?**  
A Shabbos goy is a non-Jewish person who helps with the things a Jew can't do on Shabbat, switching a light, adjusting the heating. The term is old and it's used affectionately far more often than not. Whether it lands as offensive is all in the tone and the relationship. Said warmly by someone who knows you, it's a small kindness with a name. If it ever feels off to you, that's worth a quiet word, not a silent grudge.

**Can I shake hands with Jewish people?**  
Most, yes, happily. Some observant people don't touch anyone of the opposite sex who isn't family, so now and then a hand won't come back. That's not about you and it's not about germs. If a hand doesn't come, a warm smile and a nod does the same job. No harm done, carry on.

**Do Jewish people use money on Shabbat?**  
The observant don't. No cash, no cards, no signing a bill, from Friday sundown to Saturday night. It's part of the switching-off. Which raises an obvious question about how we ever feed anyone on a Friday night, and the answer is below.

**How do we run a paid Friday Night Dinner if guests can't handle money?**  
They pay in advance. An observant guest coming for Friday night has sorted the bill before Shabbat starts, so the meal itself involves no money changing hands at the table. Nobody's dodging the cheque. They just did the paying earlier, on purpose.

**Can a Jewish person get in a car or a bus on Shabbat if they're not the one driving?**  
For the strictly observant, no, travel is out whether they're driving or not. Others draw the line differently, and plenty will happily take a lift or a bus. It's one of the clearest places you'll see the spectrum in action, two people, same faith, different answer.

**Someone praying won't respond when I speak to them. What do I do?**  
Nothing, and that's the answer. In certain prayers you're not meant to break off partway through, so if someone doesn't answer you they're not being rude, they simply can't reply yet. Wait, or come back in a minute. They'll be with you once they're done.

**What is havdalah, and why do Saturday-night events start so late?**  
Havdalah is the short ceremony that ends Shabbat, with wine, spices and a plaited candle, done once it's properly dark and the first stars are out. Since it runs on the sky rather than the clock, anything booked for after Shabbat starts when the stars do. That's also why you'll sometimes see people standing outside on a Saturday night looking up. They're not stargazing. They're waiting for Shabbat to end.

**What do people do with the candles we sell on Fridays?**  
They light them at home just before sundown to bring Shabbat in. It's one of the quiet, lovely moments of the week, usually a few minutes before everything switches off. So the candles aren't decorative. Someone is going to strike a match at a very particular time and mean it.

**Why do the holiday rentals have an urn and a hotplate?**  
Because you can't cook or switch things on once Shabbat starts, so hot food and hot water have to be sorted beforehand and kept going. The urn holds hot water for tea and coffee across the whole day. The hotplate keeps Friday's cooking warm for Saturday lunch without anyone touching a dial. Strange kit in a holiday flat until you know, then completely obvious.

**What is a Shabbat lamp?**  
A lamp with a sliding cover, so you can make a room dark or light without turning any electricity on or off. The bulb stays on the whole of Shabbat, you just open or close the shade. It's the same idea as everything else this day: set it beforehand, then leave the switches alone.

**What is an eruv, and what does it mean that we have one?**  
On Shabbat you're not meant to carry things between private and public space, which sounds minor until it means you can't carry a key, a prayer book, or a child too young to walk. An eruv is a boundary, usually made from existing walls and fences and thin wire strung between poles, that turns an enclosed area into one shared space where carrying is allowed. When an eruv is up, people can push a pram or a wheelchair to synagogue and nobody's stuck at home. When it comes down, Shabbat suddenly gets much harder for anyone with young kids or a need to carry.  There is an eruv around the BNJC, but not beyond the threshold of the property. That said, you will not offend someone if you are carrying on or off the site on Shabbat

# **What do you mean there’s a different calendar?**

The calendar most of the western world runs on is the Gregorian one, in use since 1582, built around the sun and a 365-day year. Some faiths, Jews included, run on a lunar calendar, where the sighting of a new moon marks the start of a new month.

Back in the day, a new moon was spotted and the people in charge in Jerusalem lit bonfires on the hilltops to announce it. Designated people saw those fires and lit their own to pass the word along the chain. Now you can see why Jews took to WhatsApp so quickly. It's a lot easier than standing on a hill with a torch. (It's also, as it happens, roughly why they stopped: people started lighting fake fires to mess with the signal, so the whole thing moved to messengers.)

A moon-based calendar means the holidays move. Passover is always in spring, Rosh Hashanah is always in autumn, but the exact dates drift year to year. So no, you're not going mad, the date really was different last year. And you don't have to guess. hebcal.com will tell you what's coming and when.

**Why do we close on days that seem random?**  
They're not random, they only look that way if you're reading a solar calendar. Line them up against the moon and they're perfectly regular. The whole Jewish year runs on a different clock to the one on your phone, which is why the closures land on dates that feel like they came from nowhere. They didn't.

**How do I check Hebrew dates myself?**  
hebcal.com, or the Jewish holidays layer in Google or Apple calendar. One thing to remember: everything starts the evening before the printed date, because Jewish days run from nightfall to nightfall. So a holiday "on the 25th" actually begins the night of the 24th.

**Why do festivals here run two days when Google says one?**  
Old reason. Back when the new month was announced by those hilltop fires, communities far from Jerusalem couldn't always be sure which day was the real one, so they kept two to be safe. Israel keeps one day, the diaspora kept the backup. The fires are long gone, the second day stayed.

**If the restaurant is closed, is it a fast day?**  
Usually not. Most closures are festivals, which are the opposite of a fast, they're big meals and celebration. There are two major fasts a year that stop everything, and a handful of minor ones that just quieten the lunch trade. Closed almost always means feast, not fast.

**What does fasting actually involve, and how do I act around someone who's fasting?**  
The two major fasts are twenty-five hours with no food and no water. This isn't Lent, it's the whole thing at once. The minor fasts run dawn to dusk. How to act around a faster: normally. They're fasting, not fragile. Maybe don't do a loving five-minute description of the specials, but otherwise, business as usual.

**Why do some people stop eating meat for a stretch in summer?**  
There's a mourning period in high summer, the Nine Days, where many people eat only dairy and fish. It's why the fish order spikes and sells out in early August. If the meat orders go quiet for a week or so and then come back, that's the calendar, not the kitchen.

**Why did a music or entertainment request get turned down in early summer?**  
Same shape of thing. There are mourning stretches in the calendar, the Omer and the Three Weeks, where live music is set aside. So a booking that would sail through in March gets a no in May, and it's nothing to do with the act or the room. It's the season.

**What happens to the kitchen at Passover?**  
A full changeover. Deep clean, cupboards sealed, and for a week bread simply ceases to exist on the premises. Your granola bar becomes contraband. It's the biggest kitchen event of the year and it's a lot more involved than swapping a menu, the whole place gets turned over.

**Why does everyone want the garden hut in October?**  
That's Sukkot, when meals move outside into a temporary hut called a sukkah for the week, weather permitting and often when it very much isn't. So the demand for the outdoor covered table in October isn't a quirk, it's the festival. Rain is considered part of the experience.

# **The people in the room**

At some point, two people are going to stand in front of you, both unmistakably Jewish, maybe only days apart, and do the same thing in two completely opposite ways, each certain theirs is the only way it has ever been done.

That's because Judaism is the expression of what it is to belong to the Jewish people. It's the expression of wandering, of arriving, of being exiled and scattered and told to leave, again and again, across centuries and continents. That is why there are so many ways to be Jewish.

A lot of the different expressions we see today trace back to a period called the Haskalah, the Jewish Enlightenment, in the late 1700s. The world was changing fast. Empires were falling, power was shifting to ordinary people, and Jews were leaving the ghettos and stepping into wider society for the first time. Suddenly there was a question to answer: how do you live as an Englishman, or a Frenchwoman, or a Pole, who is also a Jew, rather than a Jew who merely happens to live there? Different communities answered it differently, and out of that came the movements. They aren't rival franchises with walls between them. They're expressions, different responses to the same moment in history, and most Jews you'll meet have lived in more than one of them over a lifetime.

The textbooks will call these denominations, and that word is fine to know, but expressions is a truer term, because a Reform Jew and an Orthodox Jew are not two different religions. They're the same people, doing the same ancient thing, their own way.

**So who's actually who? Give me the short version.**  
Orthodox Jews keeps the traditional practice closely and they treat Jewish law as binding. Reform, called Progressive in Britain, keeps the tradition but reads it more flexibly for modern life. Conservative or Masorti  sit between the two, traditional in practice but open in interpretation. Those are the big three you'll meet. Nobody in that list is the fake version.

**Wait, Progressive and Reform, Conservative and Masorti, are those different things?**  
No, same thing, different names in different places. Reform in America is Progressive in Britain. Conservative in America is Masorti here. You'll hear all four words thrown around as if they're four movements. They're two, wearing different coats depending on which side of the Atlantic they're on.

**And Chabad? Is that a fourth movement?**  
No. Chabad is a Hasidic group, and Hasidism is a stream within Orthodoxy, not a separate branch alongside Reform and the rest. They're known for warmth, outreach, and turning up anywhere in the world to run a Shabbat dinner. Think of them as an expression inside the Orthodox expression, not a rival to it.

**Where does Zionism fit in all this?**  
It's not a religious movement, so it doesn't sit in the list with Orthodox and Reform. It came out of the same moment though. When Jews left the ghettos and the whole of Europe was busy with nations wanting to govern themselves, the idea that the Jewish people should also have a home of their own was the national version of that same awakening. So it belongs in this story, just not as a denomination. It cuts across all of them.

**What are the little hats?**  
A kippah, sometimes called a yarmulke. Velvet, knitted, or none at all, and the style is a quiet map of the wearer. It's worn as a small constant reminder that there's something above you. You don't need to wear one. If you find one under a table, it's not litter, set it aside somewhere respectful.

**What are the fringes hanging out of some shirts?**  
Those are tzitzit, threads on a garment worn under the shirt, there as a physical reminder of religious duties. Some people tuck them in, some let them show. Like the hats and coats, what's on the outside is telling you something about how the person practises, if you know how to read it, and there's no need to comment on it.

**Why do they rock back and forth when they pray?**  
It's called shuckling, and it's just a personal rhythm, the body moving with the words. Some people sway a lot, some not at all. It isn't choreography and nobody's told them to. Think of someone nodding along to music only they can hear.

**Why the fast mumbling, and the sudden huddle of ten men?**  
The mumbling is fluency, people who know the prayers so well they move at speed. The huddle is a minyan, the quorum of ten needed for certain prayers, being assembled wherever ten happen to be, a corridor, a corner, a spare room. If you see it forming, they're not plotting, they're one person short and about to pray.

**Wait, some rabbis wear jeans and have no beard?**  
Yes. Rabbi means teacher, not costume. The long black coat and the beard belong to certain communities, not to the job, and plenty of rabbis wear jeans, trainers, whatever. So the man in the knitted kippah and the man in the big hat can both be rabbis, and the one who looks least like your mental image might be the most senior person in the room.

**Why do some married women cover their hair, with wigs, scarves, or hats?**  
After marriage, some women cover their hair as a matter of modesty and a sign of that new chapter. The range is wide, a full wig you'd never clock, a scarf, a hat, or nothing at all. It isn't a sign of being oppressed and it isn't about looking plain, and no, they haven't shaved their heads underneath. It's a private practice with a lot of personal variation, and it means exactly as much as the woman wearing it decides it does.

**What's the quiet moment by the window on a Friday afternoon?**  
Candle lighting. Just before sunset on Friday, the Shabbat candles get lit, and that's the moment the week actually turns. It's often the calmest thirty seconds in the building all week. If you catch someone doing it, you've caught the hinge of the whole thing.

# **More about the stays**

Some of the questions in this section are the ones you'd never ask out loud. Good. That's exactly why they're here.

This is the part of the guide where it gets a little personal, because some of what you'll notice on site touches on bodies, marriage, and the private end of religious life. Why a couple asked for two separate beds when they checked in. Why someone wanted single-sex hours at a gym that doesn't have them. And yes, the one everybody's heard and nobody will say to your face, about the sheet. We'll get there.

Here's the promise for this whole section. Nothing is too awkward and no honest question makes you an idiot. The only way to get any of this wrong is to snigger or to guess. So don't snigger, don't guess, and read on. By the end you'll be able to answer a nervous guest with a straight face and a bit of warmth, which is the entire trick.

**The gym is mixed, but a guest asked about separate times. Why, and how do I answer?**  
Some observant people prefer to exercise without the opposite sex around, for the same modesty reasons that run through a lot of this section, so now and then someone will ask, usually a bit hesitantly, whether we do single-sex hours. We don't, and the honest answer is the kind one: we're a mixed gym, here's what we do offer, said warmly and without apology. Don't promise a women-only hour to be nice. A clear, friendly no beats a vague maybe that never happens.

**Why did a couple ask for two separate beds when they arrived?**  
Because some observant couples spend part of each month not sharing a bed, as part of a set of private laws around marriage and intimacy. It's a normal, planned thing for them, not a row and not a problem. You make the beds up as asked and think nothing of it. The only wrong move is treating a completely ordinary request as if it were strange.

**Why is modesty a thing, and does it mean women are lesser?**  
Modesty, tzniut, applies to men and women both, in dress and in behaviour, the idea being that not everything about you is for public display. The fair question underneath is whether it means women are treated as lesser, and the honest answer is that Jews argue about this among themselves, hard. Plenty of observant women experience it as dignity and choice, not diminishment. Others push for change from inside. Both of those are real, and you'll meet both.

**Is the gym, or the building, kosher somehow?**  
No. Kosher is about food, so a gym isn't kosher or not-kosher, the word just doesn't apply. What does carry over is Shabbat and modesty, which shape things like opening hours, the music, and what people wear. So the building isn't "kosher," but some of the same thinking still touches the parts of it that have nothing to do with the kitchen.

**Is it disrespectful to use "kosher" to mean approved or above board?**  
Not really, no. "Is that kosher?" for "is that legit?" is common and nobody's offended by it. It's a small compliment to how thorough the real thing is, that the word escaped the kitchen and came to mean trustworthy in general. Use it freely. Just know that in this building it also has its literal job.

# **Myth-busting**

No, we don't have horns, nor do we have sex through a hole in a sheet. The only time we control the media is when we hire a good PR firm, same as anyone else with something to promote.

With the big ones out of the way, let's talk about why any of this got believed in the first place, because that's the interesting part.

Most myths about Jews aren't stupid. They're old, and they usually started with a grain of something real that got stretched, mistranslated, or weaponised over a few centuries until it curdled into nonsense. None of them came from nowhere. And understanding where they came from is the fastest way to never fall for them again, and to calmly take one apart for someone else the next time it comes up in the pub. Consider it your antisemitism starter kit, in the nicest possible way.

**Do Jews have horns?**  
No, and this one has a genuinely good origin. When Moses came down the mountain, the Hebrew says his face was "karan," radiant, shining with light. But that word is closely related to the word for horns, and when the Bible was translated into Latin, the translator went with horned. Centuries of art followed, including Michelangelo's famous statue of Moses, with horns. A shining face became a horned one because of a single word choice, and later that got picked up and used against Jews, because horns had a nasty set of associations. A mistranslation that turned into a slander.

**Do Orthodox couples really have sex through a hole in a sheet?**  
No. This is one of the oldest myths going and it's flatly untrue. The likely source is a garment called a tallit, a fringed prayer shawl with an opening for the head, which somebody once saw, misunderstood, and ran with. If anything, Jewish teaching is warm about intimacy between married couples and treats it as something good, not something to be endured through soft furnishings. Believe the opposite of the myth and you're closer to the truth.

**Are Jews cheap, or obsessed with money?**  
This is the oldest and most dangerous one, so it's worth knowing where it comes from. For centuries in Europe, Jews were locked out of owning land and barred from most trades and guilds. One of the few things they were allowed to do was lend money, which Christians at the time were forbidden to do. So a whole people were forced into a job, resented for doing it, and then smeared for being good at the only thing left open to them. It's not a character trait. It's a trap, built by the people who then complained about it. Don't carry it, even secondhand.

**Do Jews secretly control the banks, the media, the weather?**  
No. This is the conspiracy family, and it's the same lie wearing different hats. It's worth saying once, plainly: there is no secret hand. What there is, is a long history of people finding it easier to blame a hidden group than to explain a complicated world. If you only remember one thing from this section, make it this one, because this is the belief that does the most damage.

**Then why are there so many Jews in Hollywood, or medicine, or law?**  
Because of that same history, turned the other way up. Locked out of land for generations, Jewish families leaned hard on the things that couldn't be confiscated: education, skills, portable professions. Then when persecution scattered them across the world, they arrived with that inheritance and no local land, no old networks, and had to build. Hollywood was a brand-new industry the establishment thought was vulgar and beneath them, so nobody minded a bunch of outsiders taking it on. They took it on. That's not a takeover. It's what people do with the doors that happen to be open when all the others are shut.

**Do Orthodox women shave their heads under the wig?**  
Almost always no. It's a myth stretched from a very small, specific custom in a few communities. For the overwhelming majority, hair-covering after marriage is a scarf, a hat, or a wig over their own hair, which is very much still there. See the hair-covering answer in the people section for what it actually means.

**Are Jews and Israelis the same thing?**  
No, and this is the distinction that prevents the most trouble at work. A Jew is a member of the Jewish people, anywhere in the world. An Israeli is a citizen of Israel, who might be Jewish, Muslim, Christian, or none of the above. And the Israeli government is a government, which Israelis argue about as loudly as anyone argues about theirs. Three different things. Keeping them separate in your head will save you a lot of awkward conversations.

**Are Israelis always very direct?**  
Often, yes, but it’s not rude. Israeli culture prizes directness even more than New Yorker’s do.  Saying the thing plainly, where British culture wraps everything in three layers of "sorry" and "if it's not too much trouble." So an Israeli guest asking for something flatly isn't being rude, they're being clear, and they'd find the British way a bit evasive. Don't take it personally. And note that this is an Israeli thing, cultural, not a Jewish one.

**A guest was rude to me. Is that a Jewish thing?**  
No. Some people are rude. That's people, not Jewishness, and there's no deeper meaning to hunt for. A Jewish person having a bad day is a person having a bad day. File it exactly where you'd file it for anyone else.

# **Jews are normal, actually**

Here's a myth so quiet nobody even says it out loud, which is what makes it the strongest one of all: that a Jewish life is a small, joyless life of rules and early nights. That the observant go home to boredom and a switched-off television and quietly endure it.

That isn't true. And where it is, it isn't Judaism doing it.

We go to concerts. We go to the cinema, we eat out, we go on holiday, we watch the same nonsense on television as everyone else and have opinions about it we'll share whether you asked or not. An Orthodox family that keeps every rule in this book to the letter will also have a favourite takeaway, a group chat full of dark jokes, and strong feelings about a football team. The observance and the ordinary life aren't fighting each other. They're the same life. Shabbat is a day off from the world, not a life sentence away from it.

The reason this matters to you is simple. The man in the black hat is not a monk. The woman covering her hair is not oppressed and joyless, and she was very possibly at the same gig you were. There are real things in any community that need to change, and they will, but anyone who explains those things as just "being Jewish" has misunderstood the whole gift. Treat the people here as full, funny, complicated human beings who happen to also be Jewish, because that's exactly what they are.

Some of the questions further down aren't about food or Shabbat at all. They're about who we actually are, and they're often the ones people are most nervous to ask, because they feel too big or too personal. Ask them anyway. I'd rather you had the answers from me, plainly, than pieced them together from the internet, which has plenty of opinions and not much warmth.

\\**Will Jewish people go to concerts, the cinema, out for dinner?**  
Yes, all of it. Observant life shapes when (not on Shabbat, not during certain mourning stretches) and sometimes where (kosher food, or working around it), but the appetite for a good night out is exactly the same as anyone's. The idea that religious means housebound is the myth this whole section exists to bury.

**Can Jews be gay, lesbian, or queer?**  
Yes. LGBTQIA+ Jews are Jews, full stop, and they're part of this community like anyone else. Within Orthodoxy there's a real and ongoing conversation about religious practice and how it squares with all this, and people land in different places on it, honestly and often painfully. But the plain answer to the plain question is yes, and you don't need to tiptoe around it.

**Could I become Jewish if I wanted to?**  
Yes. Conversion is real, and it's a serious, guided process that takes time, study, and commitment. Once you're in, you're fully in, forever, no lesser status. And here's the flip that surprises people: no one has ever tried to convert you, because Judaism doesn't go looking for recruits. We're not in the market for numbers. You have to come and knock.

**Are Jews a race, a religion, or an ethnicity?**  
Yes. All three at once, which is why the question ties people in knots. It's a religion you can practise, an ethnicity you're born into, and a people you belong to. There's an entrance, conversion, but there's no real exit, which is why someone can be a proud atheist and still be unquestionably Jewish. It breaks the usual categories because it was never built to fit them.

**Can you be half-Jewish? Can you stop being Jewish?**  
On "half," the movements answer differently, some trace it through the mother, some through either parent, so the honest answer is that it depends who you ask, and it's genuinely one of the live debates. On "stop," not really. You can walk away from every practice and belief and you're still counted in. There's a door in. There isn't really a door out.

# **The gates, the guards, and why**

You noticed the security before you'd properly got through the door. The gates. The second set of gates. Someone asking who you were before they let you in. If you've worked anywhere else in hospitality, none of that is normal, so it's fair to wonder about it, and better to have it straight than to guess.

Jewish buildings in this country are protected because they have needed to be.

Not out of drama, and not because we think we're more important than anyone else. Synagogues, schools and community centres have been targeted, here and abroad, within living memory and within the last few years. I grew up with this. The day I was born, the IRA bombed the Grand Hotel in Brighton. In 1996 my house shook one Shabbat morning as the Arndale in Manchester was blown apart. In 2005 I was visiting England when the London bombings hit the tube and the buses. And in October 2025, on Yom Kippur, the holiest day of the year, a synagogue in Manchester was attacked and people at prayer were murdered.

That last one reached me in Kansas City. The synagogue was my rabbi's family shul, the place his father goes, always early for the service. I was the one who had to tell him what had happened, on the holiest morning of the year. There is no way to make that phone call gently.

I don't tell you any of this to frighten you.  I share this because there is danger in the world, and it’s not only targetting Jewish people.  I tell you so the need for security isn’t downplayed or seen as being theatrical.  It is not suspicion. It is care. Every gate and guard on this site exists so a parent can drop a child at nursery without a knot in their stomach, and so an eighty-year-old can come to pray without watching the door. You are now part of how safe that feels to them, and a warm face at a secure door is worth more than the door.

Here is the thing I've come to believe, having been closer to terror and antisemitism in England than I ever was living in Israel. This kind of hatred is aimed at all of us, and its effect is the opposite of what it intends. We are one global family, dysfunctional at times, but that is rather the point of family, and what is meant to drive us apart tends instead to pull us closer. This building is part of that. It is a safe place, for everyone who comes through the gates. That is what the gates are for.

**Why is there so much security? The gates, the buzzers, the cameras?**  
Because Jewish sites have been targets, and the security is there so ordinary life can carry on safely underneath it. Read it as care, not suspicion. It exists so a child can be dropped at nursery and an elderly member can come to pray without fear. That's its whole job, and now yours is part of it.

**What do I do if someone wants access, or asks how to get in?**  
Be warm, and follow the site's access procedure exactly. Don't wave someone through to be friendly and don't improvise, the procedure exists for a reason and it protects everyone, including you. A kind manner and a firm process are not in conflict. You can be genuinely welcoming and still do the checks properly.

**What is the CST?**  
The Community Security Trust. They're a charity, set up in 1994, that protects the UK Jewish community, trains volunteers, works with the police, and advises places like this one. You'll hear the name, and you may see CST-trained volunteers at events or at the school gate. Their presence is a good sign, not an alarming one. It means people who know what they're doing are looking out for the community.

# **History of Jewish Brighton**

Someone is going to mention Middle Street to you, or Bear Road, or Florence Place, completely certain you know what they mean, and you'll nod along with no idea. This isn't a Jewish-Brighton version of Mornington Crescent (any Radio 4 listeners in?). So here's some of the history.

Brighton and Hove has been home to Jewish life for a very long time, more than 250 years, and the city still carries the fingerprints of it. There was a Jewish place of worship in Jew Street, so named for the Jewish traders who worked thereabouts, back in the late 1700s. The community grew, moved, and built, and in 1875 it opened Middle Street Synagogue in the centre of town, which is genuinely one of the most beautiful synagogues in Europe, said to be Brighton's finest interior after the Royal Pavilion. It's Grade II\* listed. It's still there.

The cemeteries you'll hear named are Florence Place, the oldest, given to the community in 1826, and Bear Road, now called Meadow View, which opened later as the first one filled. If people speak about those places with a particular gentleness, that's why.

Here's the part that ties it to your building. As the community moved west into Hove over the last century, its main synagogue moved with it, and eventually landed on New Church Road. Which is here. So the thread runs directly from that jewel in the middle of town to the ground you're standing on. You've joined something old, and this is its newest chapter.

If you ever get the chance to see inside Middle Street Synagogue, which isn't easy these days, say yes. It's extraordinary.

**What's Middle Street Synagogue?**  
The historic synagogue in the centre of Brighton, opened in 1875, one of the most beautiful in the country and Grade II\* listed. It isn't in full-time use now, but it opens for special occasions and it's a genuine piece of the city's heritage. If a member speaks about it fondly, they're talking about the grand old heart of Jewish Brighton.

**What are Bear Road and Florence Place?**  
The Jewish cemeteries. Florence Place is the oldest, from 1826\. Bear Road, now Meadow View, came later. That's why they tend to be mentioned quietly and with respect. If you know they're cemeteries, you'll understand the tone the moment they come up.

**Anything else I'll hear people mention?**  
A few names float around Jewish Brighton, and you don't need to memorise them, just to know they're places and pieces of history rather than in-jokes. When one comes up and you're lost, it's completely fine to say "what's that?" That's the entire spirit of this book.

# **Other Jewish bits**

This is the bit at the back for everything else. The questions that don't belong to a shift or a section, but that you've wondered about anyway. None of it will change how you do your job. All of it will make the world you're now working in make a bit more sense.

**What's the little box on the doorframe, and why do people touch it?**  
A mezuzah. There's a tiny scroll inside with a passage from the Torah, and it marks the doorway of a Jewish home or building. Many people touch it and then kiss their fingers as they pass, a small habit of affection and a reminder of what's inside. You'll see it on doorframes all over this building. Now you know it's not a doorbell.

**Why is 18 a lucky number?**  
Because in Hebrew, letters double as numbers, and the letters that spell "chai," meaning life, add up to eighteen. So eighteen quietly means life. That's why gifts and donations so often come in multiples of it, £18, £36, £180. Someone giving "chai" isn't being random, they're giving life, with the maths built in.

**What's actually written inside a Torah scroll?**  
The first five books of the Hebrew Bible, hand-written by a trained scribe on parchment, letter by letter, with no mistakes allowed. It takes about a year to write one. That's why it's dressed in a cover, handled with care, and never touched directly, people use a small pointer to follow the words. It's treated less like a book and more like the most precious object in the room, because it is.

**Do Jews believe in heaven and hell?**  
Not really, no. Although we don’t really know for sure as Judaism is famously relaxed about the specifics. There are ideas about an afterlife, but the emphasis of Jewish practice lands far more on this life, on what you do here.  If you do have an idea of heaven or hell from other faiths, it’s probably wildly different from a Jewish perspective on any possible afterlife.  If you ask ten Jews you'll get several answers and probably an argument. That's normal.

**What does "chosen people" mean, and isn't it a bit much?**  
It's easy to hear it as "better than everyone," and it doesn't mean that at all. It means chosen for a particular set of responsibilities and duties, not raised above anyone. And honestly, scratch the surface of a lot of Jewish practice and you'll find it echoes older and neighbouring traditions, ceremony, ritual, marking time. "Chosen" is a job description, not a trophy.

**Which Yiddish do I already speak?**  
More than you'd think. Schlep, chutzpah, glitch, klutz, spiel, maven, schmooze, all Yiddish, all quietly absorbed into everyday English. So the first time a member says one at you, there's a decent chance you already use it yourself without knowing where it came from. See the glossary for the fuller list.

# **Glossary, words you'll hear**

A word said to you, at speed, with total confidence that you already know it. This is where you look it up afterwards, without anyone watching.

## **The food words**

**Kosher**: food that follows Jewish dietary law. Loosely, "proper" or "above board."  
**Treif**: the opposite of kosher. Not allowed.  
**Fleishig**: meat, or a dish or a person "in the meat category." "I'm still fleishig" means recently ate meat.  
**Milchig**: dairy.  
**Parev**: neither meat nor dairy: eggs, fish, vegetables, most desserts here. The neutral category.  
**Hechsher**: the certification mark showing something is kosher.  
**Mashgiach**: the kashrut supervisor who signs off that everything is kosher.  
**Shechita**: kosher slaughter, done by a trained specialist.  
**Challah**: the plaited bread for Shabbat and festivals.  
**Kiddush**: the blessing over wine; also the food and drink after Saturday morning service.  
**Bensching**: grace after meals.

## **The time words**

**Shabbat** (Shabbos): the day of rest, Friday sundown to Saturday nightfall.  
**Yom Tov**: a festival day.  
**Chag**: a festival. "Chag Sameach" is "happy festival."  
**Erev**: the eve of. Erev Shabbat is Friday.  
**Havdalah**: the short ceremony ending Shabbat.  
**Yom Kippur**: the Day of Atonement, the holiest and most solemn day. A fast day.  
**Rosh Hashanah**: the Jewish New Year, in autumn.

## **The people and prayer words**

**Shul**: synagogue.  
**Minyan**: the quorum of ten needed for certain prayers.  
**Daven**: to pray.  
**Kippah** (yarmulke): the skullcap.  
**Tzitzit**: the fringed threads worn as a reminder of religious duties.  
**Rabbi**: a teacher and religious leader. Not a dress code.  
**Rebbetzin**: a rabbi's wife, often a leader in her own right.  
**Shomer negiah**: the practice of not touching the opposite sex outside the family.  
**Shabbos goy**: a non-Jew who helps with tasks a Jew can't do on Shabbat.

## **The life words**

**Simcha**: a celebration, and the joy of it.  
**Bar / Bat Mitzvah**: coming of age at 13 (boys) or 12 (girls).  
**Bris**: a baby boy's circumcision, on the eighth day.  
**Shiva**: the week of mourning after a death.  
**Mazel tov**: congratulations. Literally "good luck," used as "well done."  
**L'chaim**: "to life," the toast.

## **The character words (you may already use these)**

**Mensch**: a thoroughly good person. The highest compliment.  
**Chutzpah**: nerve, cheek, audacity.  
**Schlep**: to haul something a tiring distance.  
**Klutz**: a clumsy person.  
**Spiel**: a long, practised pitch.  
**Kvell**: to burst with pride.  
**Nachas**: the pride and joy you get from someone, usually your kids.  
**Nudnik**: a pest, a nag.

## **Greetings, and how not to trip**

**Shabbat Shalom**: the greeting from Friday lunchtime through Saturday.  
**Shana Tova**: "good year," around Rosh Hashanah in autumn.  
**Chag Sameach**: "happy festival," for the festivals.  
**Mazel tov**: for any happy occasion.  
**"Have an easy fast"**: what you say before Yom Kippur. Never "Happy Yom Kippur," it's a solemn day, not a happy one.  
**"May their memory be a blessing"**: what you say when someone has died.  
All of these are completely fine coming from a non-Jew, and they land warmly. Have a go.
