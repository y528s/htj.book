# How to Jew

Oct 10, 2026 · @Yosef

## Book bible

Read this before every writing session. It holds the thesis, the spine and the rules.

### Thesis

I was raised traditional, went Charedi at thirteen, rebelled into a knitted kippah, lived the best years of my life in Jerusalem, and came home having rebuilt my Judaism from the parts that held. *How to Jew* is that story, told for anyone curious about Jews, and it argues that Judaism has always lasted through practice, not doctrine: by borrowing, bending and keeping its people at the table.

### Two readers, one story

- The curious non-Jew, who may never have had a Jewish friend. The book is that friend for 300 pages.
- The story is a man rewriting his own Judaism so he has a connection: continuity, practice over doctrine, tradition over rules, and learning as an adult how much other cultures shaped Jewish life.

### Format

- Hybrid. Each chapter is a room he lived in, told as story, ending with the questions that room raises, answered in the Q&A voice.
- Every chapter opens with a scene, never an explanation.
- Q&A is never more than a third of a chapter.
- Cut any Q&A entry that no room in his life earns.

### Standing rules

- The note in his wallet is private. Never reproduce or paraphrase it. The refusal can appear on the page.
- No Second Lebanon War story.
- Coming out lives in its own chapter. It is not part of the pitch or the thesis.
- Dictated material is raw. Proofread and guide; do not add words, images or details he didn't give.
- Voice: his Speaking From Within essay voice. Short sentences, full grammar, wit inside the argument, no standup bits. No em dashes. British spelling.
- Every Jewish tradition is treated as valid. There is no one way to Jew.
- Zionism is his conviction and is argued, not asserted. Jewish anti-Zionists get their best case in his words before his answer.
- Avoid factual overclaims ("the only indigenous people", "freedom of everything").
- Former wife and children: show them their parts before anyone else sees them. Never name the strictly Orthodox school in Manchester; describe it.

## Chapter 1: The Break-Fast (v0.2)

*Recovered draft v0.2 (19 July 2026), placed verbatim. Needs a pass: em dashes out, and the "how this book works" section still describes the old calendar structure.*

The first time — and, so far, the only time — I have ever climbed into a fire engine, it was parked outside my grandmother's art deco flat.

She lived on the sixth floor, with views of the Sussex Downs on one side and the seafront stretching for miles on the other. The wallpaper was gold and velvet stripe, and the flat was a constant of my life until the day she died: always open, whether for a cup of tea, a glass of scotch in front of the evening news, or one of the occasions when trestle tables filled the room and the room somehow agreed to hold them.

So there was nothing strange about the fact that on this particular night, fifty or so of us — aunts, uncles, cousins, friends, neighbours from the building, many of them Jewish too — were squeezed around her table at the end of Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, for the meal that breaks the fast. There was also nothing strange, at least to us, about the fact that only a handful of the fifty had actually fasted, and fewer still had been anywhere near a synagogue. The meal was not a reward for observance. The meal was the observance. Nobody at that table would have put it that way, and nobody needed to.

That was when the fire alarm went off.

It became clear quite quickly that this was not a drill. Fifty of us left the table and filed down the stairs — hundreds of them, it felt like, six storeys' worth — and out into a night already washed in red flashing lights.

My mother and my uncle, without discussion, went back in. They took it upon themselves to go door to door through the building, helping the elderly and the infirm down those same stairs. I should mention that my mother had, at this point, not eaten or drunk anything for twenty-five hours. I was upset about this at the time — not the fasting, the going back in — and registered my protest in the way children do, which is to say I was ignored. My cousins and I were left in the care of the firemen managing the scene, one of whom lifted me into the cab of the engine, which is how I came to make the climb I have never repeated since.

My grandmother, for her part, also had priorities. Before evacuating, she went to the safe and collected her jewellery. Then she put on a fur coat, to keep warm, and came down all six floors dressed for the occasion.

The fire, it turned out, never spread beyond a single flat. Hours later we climbed back up, took our places, and resumed. My mother, who had spent the evening of the holiest day of the year carrying her neighbours' arms down a stairwell, finally broke her twenty-five-hour fast on an apple.

That was my grandmother's flat, and this is my family.

My grandmother described herself, without any sense of contradiction, as a Jewish atheist. She helped fundraise and establish the Reform synagogue in Brighton — and then left the congregation because it wasn't religious enough. If that sentence doesn't make sense to you, good. You are exactly the reader I wrote this book for, and by the end of it, it will make perfect sense, and you will have met a hundred people like her.

We were, in those years, chicken soup on Friday night Jews. Roast chicken and potatoes Jews. Chopped liver Jews. My great-grandmother's chicken soup recipe is still made in our family's homes, generations on, which as far as I can tell makes it the most successfully transmitted piece of Jewish law in our entire lineage.

My parents divorced when I was five or six, and — in the way these things sometimes go — both became far more observant afterwards. I spent my formative years in an ultra-Orthodox community in Manchester, black hat and side curls, being taught a Judaism that you did because you did it, not because you understood it. When my teenage rebellion arrived, it took the form you would least expect: I became a modern Orthodox Jew. I kept the faith, kept kosher, kept Shabbat — and, gasp, watched films and listened to the radio. It was not until my sixteenth birthday that a television entered my house, and only then because my grandmother had inspected the situation and formally ruled me a deprived child.

Manchester was so Jewish that I did not make my first non-Jewish friend until after I had left — working in a call centre in Jerusalem. I want you to sit with the geography of that sentence for a moment. I had to move to the Middle East to meet a gentile.

Fast forward to my forties: a divorced father of three, carrying a hundred and one questions about faith and practice and sexuality and prayer and tradition, and none of the answers I had been raised on. I had the enormous luck of finding a rabbi who got all of it — who could tell the difference between the "sacred" and the actually sacred, who approached religion with logic and without dismissing science or history, and who, not to overstate it, saved my relationship with our faith.

Like the generations before me, I have spent my life inside Jewish communities — sometimes as part of their infrastructure, sometimes just by opening my own front door and building the village I wanted around me. And along the way I have learned something from every flavour of Jew there is.

Here is what all of it taught me, and here is the premise of this book.

There is no one way to Jew. My mother's way, that night, was the stairwell. My grandmother's way was the fur coat — and she would want you to know the fur coat was also correct. The black hats of Manchester have their way; the Reform congregation my grandmother founded and then flounced out of has theirs; and none of them is the fake version of somebody else's real thing. Nobody is doing this wrong on purpose. Judaism has been evolving for three thousand years, and every Jew you will ever meet is standing somewhere on that moving map, doing their honest best with the coordinates they were given.

I didn't always believe this. For years I was sure there was exactly one right way to be a Jew, and that it came with a black hat, which I happened to be wearing. It took me most of my life, and every room in this book, to unlearn it.

And I know what it is like to stand outside the glass, because I once stood on the other side of it. I once knew as little about you as you may know about us. So let me say the thing this whole book exists to say: you don't need to be afraid to be yourself around us. You are allowed to ask. If you are worried a question might be offensive, the worry itself is usually proof that it's fine. We are people — with our quirks, our arguments, our recipes that outlive our beliefs — and most of us, honestly, are quite a nice bunch anyway.

A quick word about how this book works, and then we'll get going.

The Jewish year does not run on your calendar. It runs on the moon, which is why the holidays seem to wander around your scheduling app like they've lost their keys — so rather than marching from January to December, we'll walk the year the way we actually live it, starting in September, where it begins. If you only need to know why half your office is out in October, there's a cheat sheet for that; the rest of you can take the scenic route with me.

Between the parts of this book you'll find pages of rapid-fire questions — the ones people are usually too embarrassed to ask out loud, answered the way I'd answer a text from a friend. Can you invite us to your Christmas party? (Yes. We might even come.) Do all rabbis have beards? (No. Stay tuned.) Nothing asked in good faith is off the table; that is roughly the founding principle of my entire life.

And in the practical chapters — food, Shabbat, weddings, mourning — I'll finish with the part you can act on: what to bring, what to say, what never to say in a house of mourning. Whoever you are — colleague, friend, neighbour, in-law-to-be, or a Jew reading over someone's shoulder to check whether I got it right — you're who I wrote this for.

How you Jew is yours. How you come to know us — that part, I can help with. Come on up. The kettle's on, the scotch is open, and if the fire alarm goes off, you'll want to see what my grandmother wears down the stairs.

### Questions this room raises

**Why are there so many kinds of Jew?** Because Judaism is the expression of what it is to be part of the Jewish ethnic group. It's the expression of walking through the desert for forty years and ending up in the Promised Land, only to be chased out and have to roam around the diaspora. To be persecuted in Europe, to be told to go back to where you came from, only to be told by the world to leave again once you get there.

Much like many aspects of life, development, sexuality and intellect, faith sits on a spectrum, and everyone belongs. And right or left of that spectrum isn't better or worse than anyone else's way of doing things. Part of the reason for all these different Jewish expressions comes from a period called the Haskalah, or Enlightenment. In the 1770s there was tremendous change going on in the world. Empires were falling, monarchies were collapsing, and power was shifting to the people. Jews were leaving the ghettos, and at that intersection with society, they had to find an identity. As a result, Jewish people found ways to live their lives as English, Czech, French, Polish, Russian, Moroccan, American, Spanish and Portuguese people who were also Jews, rather than being Jews who happened to live in those places. So the movements we see today, Orthodox, Reform, Conservative, Progressive, Chabad and Zionist, all have their roots in different responses to the same historical context. Each of these sects had intellectuals and philosophers spreading their own How to Jew manuscripts, rewriting the rules for a new time, and traditions started forming.

There is an old joke about a Jew rescued from a desert island who is asked why he built two synagogues. One is the one he goes to. The other is the one he would never set foot in.

**Give me the short version.** Orthodox Jews keep traditional practice closely and treat Jewish law as binding. Reform keeps the tradition but reads it more flexibly for modern life. Conservative, or Masorti, sits between: traditional in practice, open in interpretation.

**Are Progressive and Reform, or Conservative and Masorti, different things?** Mostly the same things in different coats. Reform in America is close to Progressive in Britain, where Reform and Liberal Judaism have joined. Conservative in America is Masorti here.

**Is Chabad a fourth movement?** No. Chabad is a Hasidic group, and Hasidism is a stream within Orthodoxy. They are known for warmth, outreach, and turning up anywhere in the world to run a Shabbat dinner.

## Chapter 2: The Staircase (v0.1)

Compiled from the guide's preface and opening section. Brackets mark gaps.

I could see the roof of my school from my bedroom window, and I was still late nearly every morning. We lived on Westbourne Villas, close enough that the walk should have been impossible to get wrong, and that plot of land was the whole of my small world. There was a school there then. It has since been knocked down to build something bigger, but for a few years in the beginning it was where I learned, more or less, how to be a person.

You walked in through the double doors, after lining up in height order when the bell rang to start the day, and the library was on your left. It smelled musty, like books that had been there longer than our young minds could ever have imagined. That is where we held morning prayers, and where I learned to find my way around a prayer book, which is a genuine skill. It is a bit like learning to read sheet music, except everyone is singing already and nobody shows you the page. On your right was the Mark Luck Hall, which led a triple life: karate club in the week, the shul hall for kiddush every Saturday, and Sunday club and youth group after that. One room, three completely different sets of people, each certain it was theirs. I have thought about that hall a lot since. It turns out to be a fairly good description of Jewish life in general.

The staircase, which I remember as grand, though I was small and may not be a reliable witness, is where Mrs Efune declined to tie my shoelace. I had asked her to, coming in from break, fully expecting the service. She told me, kindly, that she thought I could manage it myself. Then she waited. She did not do it for me and she did not hurry me, while I sat and fought with the thing. When I finally got it she applauded as if I had done something remarkable. Which, to me, I had.

There was hot chocolate from Rabbi Efune in the kitchen the morning it snowed. There were the birthday lunches Mrs Stark made, so that one day a year the food was yours and everyone knew it. The thick wedge of birthday cake with white icing and sprinkles was only served when one of us had a birthday. And there was the spelling test where a very sharp pencil somehow ended up lodged in my forearm, and Mrs Smith calmly helped me get it out, just as we were being asked to spell the word "synagogue". Given what I ended up doing with my life, I have decided to read that as a sign.

This plot of land and the people on it didn't just teach me how to Jew. They taught me how to read two alphabets, how to respect the people around me, how to multiply numbers and grow cress on a windowsill. And I turned out fine, speaking two languages and tying my own shoes, even if I still can't read an analogue clock.

Our village in Brighton was a village of single mothers. I still don't know whether it was the school, thirty children in a building that felt like ours, or the mothers themselves, holding each other up. But a handful of families began to take on more, and at almost exactly the same pace.

We had always kept a kosher home. Now we walked to shul every week instead of taking the bus. The television went, because a television was, apparently, a marker of where you stood. And one by one, the families who were taking on more left Brighton and Hove.

I have often wondered what that cost the community. What it would look like now if we had all stayed, and raised another generation of Orthodox Jews by the sea.

But we didn't.

The rabbi who nurtured all that growth believed what many rabbis believe in places without enough Jewish schools (I am looking at you, Overland Park): if you want your children to grow, you move. To London. To Manchester. To elsewhere.

In 1993, when I was eight, elsewhere was Manchester.

### Questions this room raises

**Am I allowed to ask questions about Judaism at all?** Yes. Please do. Jews are curious by nature; the Talmud is volumes of argument, some of it settled, much of it written down for the sake of it. The only rule is the obvious one: ask with respect, and read the room. A question that is perfect over a quiet coffee is a bad one to shout across a busy Friday service.

**What if I say the wrong word?** You will, and it does not matter. Hebrew is hard, and half these words shift depending on whose grandmother is saying them. A warm, mangled attempt beats a nervous silence every time.

**Is "Jew" a rude word?** No. It is just the word, and Jewish people use it about themselves all day. It can sound risky, usually because the last time you heard it out loud it was in the wrong mouth. Said plainly and warmly, it is exactly right.

**Is there anything I shouldn't ask?** Barely one thing. Don't audit people. "You drove here on Saturday but you keep kosher?" feels like curiosity and lands like an accusation. Everyone sits somewhere on a long, personal spectrum of observance, and none of them owes you a scorecard. Ask what kosher is and you will get a warm answer. Ask someone to justify the gap between two of their own choices and you have become their least favourite relative.

## Chapter 3: The Black Hat (v0.1)

*v0.1, polished from dictation, 12 October 2026.*

Before I tell you how the ultra-Orthodox world began to close around me, I owe Manchester this: our arrival was nothing short of warm.

On my first day at my new school, I sat down next to a boy who shared my birthday. Not just the date. We were born within minutes of each other. I have met plenty of people who share my birthday since, and it has never stopped delighting me, but at eight I thought it was a miracle.

When I got home that afternoon, my mother had found a parcel on the doorstep of our still-unpacked house. Bread, milk, tea, biscuits. It was from my birthday twin's aunt, who lived forty-five seconds away.

That is Manchester. A village of love and kindness. And countless stringencies that people have adopted as law.

There is a word for those: chumrah. A chumrah is an extra layer, a fence built around the law so you never get near its edge. Some are ancient and beautiful. But many of the people living inside them do not realise they are custom, not commandment. Elsewhere, I was learning, came with a lot of fences.

When my bar mitzvah came, there was a gemach, a community fund, to pay for my black hat. Nobody asked whether I wanted one. It was assumed that a religious adult of thirteen would naturally want a Borsalino on his head. So I got one.

It was a confusing age. Religious doctrine, changing schools, questions I could not say out loud, and a sexuality I was only beginning to notice. I bounced between schools, miserable.

The hat was chosen in 1997, the year the first Harry Potter book quietly appeared. I didn't pick the books up until the fourth one came out, three years later. But the moment I read about Ollivanders wand shop, I was transported straight back to that tiny hat shop.

I still don't know whether it was an office in someone's home or the back room of a shop. An evening visit had been arranged by the man sponsoring my hat. My mother was not allowed to come. The separation of men and women was one of those stringencies so ordinary that nobody saw it as sexist. They saw it as holy.

I walked in nervous. Hats filled Billy bookcases from floor to ceiling, and hat boxes with French descriptions and Italian names filled every nook and cranny. My head was measured. The brim was discussed. And, like magic, the hat seller knew exactly what I needed, and, even more surprisingly, exactly where it lived in his organised chaos.

I walked out with a hat box and a hat that seemed to have been waiting for me. I remember the feel of the felt, the brand name stamped inside, and the ribbon sitting just so at the base of the crown.

At first, the hat was pride. I belong here. You accept me. My sleeves were the right length, my shirt was the right colour, and the hat said the rest.

But over time, the hat began to hide the kippah underneath it. First black velvet. Then suede. And then knitted, the symbol of my teenage rebellion, and of who I was becoming.

Eventually the hat went back in its box. It never came out again.

### Questions this room raises

**What are the little hats?** A kippah, or yarmulke. It is a small, constant reminder that there is something above you. It is also, whether the wearer means it or not, a badge. Jews can read each other's heads from across a room, and I have worn most of the map.

**How do I read a kippah?** Roughly, and with exceptions everywhere, like this:

- **Black velvet**, often under a black hat: the Charedi and yeshiva world. Serious, traditional, uniform.
- **Black suede or leather:** still acceptable in the frum world, but a little edgier. When I switched to suede, it still said I prayed three times a day. It might also say you had a fast car, or that you were hooking up after Maariv. It was a step away from what velvet stood for.
- **Knitted (kippah srugah):** religious Zionism, Bnei Akiva, Modern Orthodoxy. Colour, pattern and size say more again. A very large white one with a tassel tells you something quite different from a small dark one clipped to the back of the head.
- **Embroidered pillbox:** Bukharan in origin, now worn far and wide, and popular in progressive synagogues.
- **Satin, with names printed inside:** the souvenir from someone's wedding or bar mitzvah. Every Jewish home has a drawer of them.
- **On a woman:** common in Reform and Liberal communities, rare elsewhere.
- **None at all:** plenty of Jews, including some Orthodox men outside prayer, and most Reform Jews most of the time.

And yes, it is held on with a hair clip. Nobody has found a better way.

**What are the fringes hanging out of some shirts?** Tzitzit: threads on a garment worn under the shirt, a physical reminder of religious duties. Some tuck them in, some let them show. Many connect the knots to the 613 commandments.

**Why do they rock back and forth when they pray?** It is called shuckling. It is a personal rhythm, the body moving with the words. Think of someone nodding along to music only they can hear.

**Why the fast mumbling, and the sudden huddle of ten men?** The mumbling is fluency. The huddle is a minyan, the quorum of ten needed for certain prayers, assembled wherever ten happen to be. If you see it forming, they are not plotting. They are one person short.

**Do all rabbis have beards?** No. Rabbi means teacher, not costume. The long black coat and the beard belong to certain communities, not to the job. The man in jeans might be the most senior person in the room.

**Why do some married women cover their hair?** As a matter of modesty and a sign of a new chapter. A wig you would never clock, a scarf, a hat, or nothing at all. It is not a sign of oppression, and no, they have not shaved their heads underneath. It means as much as the woman wearing it decides it does.

**Can I shake hands with a Jewish person?** Most, happily. Some observant people do not touch anyone of the opposite sex outside the family, so now and then a hand will not come back. It is not about you. A warm smile and a nod does the same job.

## Chapter 4: The Knitted Kippah (v0.1)

*v0.1, polished from dictation, 12 October 2026.*

I went to both a strictly Orthodox school in Manchester and King David, a big Jewish school with children from every kind of home. I may not be the only person who went to both. But I am almost certain I am the only one who ping-ponged between them, back and forth, as my emotional, physical and religious development collided.

At the first, I had to conform. To the rules, and to the stringencies. If you are reading this book to learn about Judaism, it will sound absurd that a boy could be thrown out of school for wearing a knitted kippah. But this was a corner of the community at the far end of the map, and that was the rule.

So I had two. Every afternoon, once I was far enough from the school gates and out of sight of my teachers and classmates, I would take the acceptable one off my head and put on the one I felt like myself in. I don't remember when it started, or even where that kippah came from.

I was not only hiding from a rule. Some of those teachers, had they caught me, would have had no trouble tearing into me with words. None of them ever laid a hand on me. But in that place, the fear that one might was always in the room.

I am still not sure whether I should be proud that this was the height of my teenage rebellion, or sad that I lived in a world where it counted as one.

And then I found Bnei Akiva. Religious Zionism made sense to me in a way nothing had before: Jewish continuity, Jewish pride, a people deciding its own future, and none of it built out of fences. By my teens I was one of a handful of Shabbat-observant kids in an enormous school. But I had found a Judaism that let me go to concerts and watch films without feeling ashamed.

I don't remember exactly when I first went. I remember what it did. I stopped being the frightened boy who half expected a thunderbolt from the sky if he mispronounced a blessing, and I started to become a leader.

For a number of years, that building was the happiest place in my life. Nobody asked me to pore over the Talmud for four hours a day or recite the oral law from memory. Instead, Judaism came alive. It tied our ancient customs to the world outside the window, and it made me proud of who I was, in the years before Zionism became a word people used as an insult. Questions were welcome. My Judaism felt intellectually honest, and worth something. Not stuffy, and not restrictive.

It gave me highs and lows. Friendships that are still strong today, and moments of shock and loss that bonded us even tighter.

But here is what has never left me. To this day, when I stand in any synagogue in the world on a Friday night and the congregation begins Kabbalat Shabbat, the songs that welcome Shabbat in, I am back in the octagonal shul at Bnei Akiva in Salford, where I fell in love with those tunes and nobody judged how I sang them.

Floor-to-ceiling windows towered over us. At night they held the stars. At morning services, the sun poured in. In that room our voices and our prayers became one, whatever our politics, whichever school we went to, man or woman.

It was the first room where I saw women ask to dance with the Torah on Simchat Torah, and saw the answer come back yes.

It was the room that taught teenagers to love who they were, not run from it.

Both corners of Jewish Manchester were doing what they believed was right, building community and protecting Jewish continuity. One did it with the fear of banishment. The other did it with song, and a sense of belonging.

I can't point to one moment when Israel became the plan. It came in slowly, like a tide. Between Yad Achim, the anthem of our movement, and Hatikvah, the anthem of our nation, I sang my way into a sense of purpose and pride.

One Yom Ha'atzmaut, Israel's Independence Day, our school hall, big enough to seat a thousand students for assembly, was turned into a papier-mâché map of Israel.

We were split into groups of four, and each group built a piece of the land. It felt fitting. We had grown up on stories of coins dropped into blue JNF boxes and sent off to buy the land, parcel by parcel, from Arab landowners willing to sell.

My group got the Golan Heights. Not long before, I had hiked the Banias and eaten shawarma in Katzrin. Now I was shaping those same hills out of newspaper and paste.

None of it came only from books. Bnot sherut, young Israeli women doing national service, came to our school as emissaries, and they never missed a beat. They made us fall in love with every corner of the land.

### Questions this room raises

**Is "Zionist" a bad word?** No, though the last few years have done a number on it. Underneath the noise it means the belief that the Jewish people, like other peoples, have a right to a home. It comes in a dozen flavours that argue with each other endlessly, and for most Jews it is not a hot political stance. It is woven in.

**Where does Zionism fit?** It is not a denomination, but you cannot pull it out of the religion. Open a prayer book and it points to Jerusalem. Most festivals and most lifecycle moments reach toward Zion somewhere in the words. The longing is as old as the tradition. Modern Zionism grew out of the same moment as the denominations: when the nations of Europe were waking up to self-government, Jews asked the same question of themselves. That is why a Reform Jew and an Orthodox Jew who agree on almost nothing else will be seen celebrating Israel at the same event.

### Questions this room raises

**What actually is Shabbat?** A full day of stopping, in honour of the day God rested after making the world. Once a week the world is told to wait.

**Shabbat or Shabbos?** Both. Same word, two pronunciations. Which one someone uses tells you where they are from more than what they believe.

**Why no electricity?** Shabbat pauses acts of making, and completing a circuit is treated as one. So lights, ovens and switches are set before sundown and left alone. It looks like a rule about electricity. It is really a rule about not building things on your day off.

**Why does the lift stop at every floor on a Saturday?** Shabbat mode, so a person can ride without pressing a button. A piece of engineering built entirely so someone can have a day off from buttons.

**Why did a Jewish friend ask me to turn on a light?** Because they can't and you can, and asking is allowed where doing it is not. They may hint: "it's dark in here". You are free to say yes or no.

**What is a Shabbos goy, and is it offensive?** A non-Jew who helps with what a Jew can't do on Shabbat. The term is old and usually affectionate. Whether it lands badly is all tone and relationship.

**Do Jewish people use money on Shabbat?** The observant don't. No cash, no cards, no signing a bill. Paid dinners are paid in advance.

**Can someone ride in a car on Shabbat?** For the strictly observant, no. Others draw the line differently. Two people, same faith, different answer.

**Why won't someone praying answer me?** In some prayers you don't break off partway. Wait a minute. They will be with you.

**What is havdalah?** The short ceremony that ends Shabbat, with wine, spices and a plaited candle, once three stars are out. That is why people stand outside on a Saturday night looking up.

**Why an urn and a hotplate?** Because you can't cook once Shabbat starts. Hot water and Friday's food are kept going without anyone touching a dial.

**What is a Shabbat lamp?** A lamp with a sliding cover, so you can make a room dark or light without switching anything.

**What is an eruv?** On Shabbat you don't carry between private and public space, which sounds minor until it means you can't push a pram to synagogue. An eruv is a boundary, usually walls and thin wire on poles, that turns an area into one shared space where carrying is allowed.

### Questions this room raises

**Is kosher food blessed by a rabbi?** No. Kosher is a system: rules about ingredients, about keeping things apart, and someone checking it happens. The rabbi is the auditor, not a wizard.

**What makes meat kosher?** The animal has to be on the permitted list: cud-chewing with split hooves, or a named list of birds. It's kinship — which, if you happen to be dating one of us, is the most useful thing this book will tell you: you are not taking on someone's beliefs, you are joining someone's people, and the family dinner will always matter more than the theology conversation.

Here is the one feature that breaks every category you'd try to put us in: the door does open — inward. A convert arrives with none of my Levantine cheek-swab and becomes fully, completely, irrevocably a Jew. Not an associate member. Not a Jew-with-an-asterisk. In, forever — no-exit clause included; the door swings one way for everybody. We are, as far as I can tell, the only "race" in human history that issues naturalization papers. King David's own great-grandmother was a convert named Ruth. We put her book in the Bible and read it standing up.

A religion that doesn't require belief — you met my grandmother, the synagogue-founding atheist; a friend of mine once put it perfectly: Christianity is for the followers of Christ, but Judaism is how Jews express who we already are, which is why there's no pope, no leader to follow, and a thousand ways to do it. An ethnicity, in a dozen regional flavours that get their own chapter. And a race with an entrance and no exit. Hold all three at once and you finally have the right map.

Now you can hear what the man by the truck actually said. Not *I accept you* — *I've cleared you*. You pass. And passing is not belonging; it's a conditional visa, revocable at a glance — and one my family has held before. We passed in Spain, right up until we didn't. Jews were barred from the clubs and quotas and neighbourhoods of "white" England and America within living memory; the whiteness on offer now is recent, partial, and — every Jew quietly knows — returnable.

I don't even have to reach back for proof. In the very week I write this sentence, the prime minister of Malaysia announced that any Israeli found in his country will be deported on sight. In London, the leader of the Green Party — himself Jewish — has backed a public call for the government to keep records of British nationals who served in Israel's army, with extra screening at the border; its authors insist it has nothing to do with Jews, while the Board of Deputies calls it a way of marking people out for intimidation. Wherever you stand on any of it, notice the shape: lists, borders, papers. The oldest suspicions never die. They just update their vocabulary.

Besides, the premise fails on contact: walk into any synagogue in Jerusalem and try to sort the room by your racial categories. You'll give up by the second row.

He looked at me carefully for three days and decided I was acceptable. What I'd thought we were doing, over the branches and the tea and the Big Gulps, was becoming known.

The tree, in the end, became firewood. It burns in my firepit on Saturday nights while we make Havdalah, the little ceremony that ends Shabbat — the tornado that started all of this now keeping my tribe warm in the garden.

He asked every question right, that man, for three days — until the one he didn't know he was asking. This chapter exists so that you never have to answer it the way he did. You now know what we are. All of it. Yes.

### Questions this room raises

**Are Jews a race, a religion or an ethnicity?** All three at once, which is why the question ties people in knots. A religion you can practise, an ethnicity you are born into, a people you belong to. There is an entrance, conversion, but no real exit, which is why a proud atheist can still be unquestionably Jewish.

**Can you be half-Jewish?** The movements answer differently. Some trace it through the mother, some through either parent. It is a live debate.

**Can you stop being Jewish?** Not really. There's a door in. There isn't really a door out.

**Could I become Jewish?** Yes. Conversion is a serious, guided process. Once you are in, you are fully in, forever. And nobody has ever tried to convert you, because Judaism doesn't go looking for recruits. The first stop is a conversation with a rabbi.

## Chapter 9: The Year I Stayed Home (v0.1)

As a young child in Brighton, we would go to Middle Street Synagogue. It is shuttered now, but as a listed building it has become an asset that anchors the history of what Jewish Brighton once was, rather than the centre of Jewish life today.

Middle Street was active and held services when I was a child, and one Yom Kippur we were told to stay inside the building, as someone had climbed onto the roof in an attempt to disrupt the service.

My sukkah is a special place. My birthday on the Hebrew calendar is the first day of Sukkot, and it's the reason I have an affinity with my Hebrew birthday. I always host the first night of Sukkot and fill the four walls with friends and family. Miraculously, there are always enough seats and enough food.

My guests walk out to the temporary hut in my garden, draped in tapestries for a sense of warmth and welcome, where two flags are proudly hung. One is the flag of the State of Israel, which also flies outside my Kansas home. The other is the flag of Nova, to honour and remember those whose lives were brutally taken on October 7. I'm not much of a dancer, but I do deeply believe that "we will dance again" is etched into our culture as deeply as Am Yisrael Chai.

Sukkot honours the protection the Jewish people had while wandering in the desert: a pillar of cloud by day and a pillar of fire by night.

It probably comes as no surprise that I take Pesach pretty seriously too. I have a manuscript I add to every year and bring to the seder. The stories, customs and unique traditions I like to bring to the table now run to more than sixty printed pages, and I have a dream of publishing a Haggadah one day. It's a project that started in 2007, when I hosted my first seder, something I've done every year since, bar two.

Two come to mind.

I love a lengthy seder, rich in dialogue and storytelling. We don't just read the text in front of us. After all, that's just boring and laborious. Channelling my inner Priya Parker, I curate the evening and give a lot of thought to the prep.

This is why my karpas isn't parsley dipped in salt water. It's a Mediterranean mezze, with roast potatoes and parsnips, Moroccan carrots, and leek and potato patties, which I borrowed from my Rosh Hashanah tradition one year because I confused my ritual foods and holidays, and it stuck. I have a vague memory of once reading that karpas used to be an appetiser course, and I lean into it. The dialogue and traditions flow, and the laughter follows, when people aren't hungry or looking at their watches, counting the pages until dinner.

My second much-loved tradition is my fifth cup of wine at the end of the seder. But you'll have to wait for my Haggadah, or ask me, to find out more about that.

### Questions this room raises

**Why do Jewish holidays move every year?** The Jewish calendar runs on the moon. A new moon starts a month, and a leap month every few years keeps the festivals in their seasons. Passover is always spring, Rosh Hashanah always autumn, but the dates drift. Back in the day, a new moon was announced by bonfires lit from hilltop to hilltop out of Jerusalem. Now you can see why Jews took to WhatsApp so quickly. Every day starts at nightfall, so a holiday "on the 25th" begins the night of the 24th.

**Why two days of festival outside Israel?** Communities far from Jerusalem couldn't be sure which day the fires had announced, so they kept two to be safe. The fires are long gone. The second day stayed.

**What does fasting involve?** The two major fasts, Yom Kippur and Tisha B'Av, are twenty-five hours with no food and no water. Minor fasts run dawn to dusk. How to act around someone fasting: normally. They are fasting, not fragile. Before Yom Kippur, say "have an easy fast", never "happy Yom Kippur".

**Why do some people stop eating meat in summer, or turn down live music in spring?** Mourning stretches: the Omer in spring, the Three Weeks and the Nine Days in high summer.

**What happens to a kitchen at Passover?** A full changeover. Deep clean, cupboards sealed, and for eight days bread ceases to exist. Your granola bar becomes contraband.

**Why eat in a hut outside in October?** Sukkot. Meals move into a temporary hut for a week. Rain is considered part of the experience.

**Do Jews believe in heaven and hell?** Not really in the way you might picture. There are ideas about an afterlife, but Jewish practice lands far more on this life and what you do here. Ask ten Jews and you'll get several answers and an argument.

**What does "chosen people" mean?** Chosen for a set of responsibilities, not raised above anyone. A job description, not a trophy.

## Chapter 10: The Gates (v0.1)

You notice the security at a Jewish building before you are properly through the door. The gates. The second set of gates. Someone asking who you are before they let you in. It is fair to wonder about it.

Security is a funny thing, and it's a lot more serious than security theatre that exists purely as a deterrent. In recent years alone there has been the burning of Jewish medical vehicles in London (it's worth noting that the organisation, Hatzola, serves Jewish and non-Jewish people when they call for help, and often arrives much faster than 999 can, thanks to its very high density of volunteers), and the Heaton Park attack on Yom Kippur.

Unfortunately, terrorism is real, and unlike the IRA, who would often politely call ahead with a warning, which saved lives in Manchester in 1996, such etiquette no longer exists. Not so at the Grand Hotel in Brighton in 1984. I spent my entire life being told it blew up the day I was born. I was less than twenty-four hours old when it happened, but it happened in my home town. Given that Jewish mothers have a flair for exaggeration, and the emotions that flow after having your first child could justify a dramatic memory, it doesn't take a professional to suggest the story of this event became some sort of core implanted memory in my psyche, and could have influenced how I feel in public spaces.

Some synagogues and Jewish buildings require all guests to register their visit ahead of time, so background checks can be performed in advance of their arrival. Sadly, Dara Horn's book People Love Dead Jews isn't just a catchy title. It's a reality we face every day.

I grew up with this. In 1996, my house shook one Shabbat morning as the Arndale in Manchester was blown apart. In October 2025, on Yom Kippur, the holiest day of the year, a synagogue in the same city was attacked and people at prayer were murdered.

That last one reached me in Kansas City. The synagogue was my rabbi's family shul, the place his father goes, always early for the service. I was the one who had to tell him what had happened, on the holiest morning of the year. There is no way to make that phone call gently.

I do not tell you this to frighten you. I tell you so the gates are not mistaken for drama. They are not suspicion. They are care. They exist so a parent can drop a child at nursery without a knot in their stomach, and so an eighty-year-old can come to pray without watching the door.

Having been closer to terror and antisemitism in England than I ever was living in Israel, here is what I have come to believe. This hatred is aimed at all of us, and its effect is the opposite of what it intends. We are one global family, dysfunctional at times, but that is rather the point of family. What is meant to drive us apart tends to pull us closer.

### Questions this room raises

**What is the CST?** The Community Security Trust, a charity set up in 1994 that protects the UK Jewish community and trains volunteers. Its presence is a good sign, not an alarming one.

No, we don't have horns, nor do we have sex through a hole in a sheet. The only time we control the media is when we hire a highly qualified PR firm, the same as everyone else with something to promote.

**Do Jews have horns?** No, and the origin is genuinely interesting. When Moses came down the mountain, the Hebrew says his face was "karan", radiant. The word is close to the word for horns, and the Latin translator went with horned. Centuries of art followed, Michelangelo's Moses included. A mistranslation that turned into a slander.

**Are Jews cheap, or obsessed with money?** For centuries in Europe, Jews were barred from owning land and from most trades. Lending money was one of the few jobs left open, and one Christians were forbidden to do. A whole people were forced into a job, resented for it, then smeared for being good at it. It is not a trait. It is a trap built by the people who then complained about it.

**Do Jews secretly control the banks or the media?** No. This is the same lie in different hats. There is no secret hand, only the long habit of blaming a hidden group for a complicated world. If you remember one thing from this chapter, make it this.

**Then why so many Jews in Hollywood, medicine or law?** The same history the other way up. Locked out of land, families leaned on what couldn't be confiscated: education, skills, portable professions. Hollywood was a new industry the establishment thought vulgar, so nobody minded outsiders taking it on. That is not a takeover. It is what people do with the doors left open.

**A Jewish person was rude to me. Is that a Jewish thing?** No. Some people are rude. That is people, not Jewishness.

## Chapter 11: The Bedroom (v0.1)

Although I share my birthday with National Coming Out Day, my journey reconciling and accepting my own sexuality isn't something that happened on one single day. It wasn't one single incident or occasion that led me to an aha moment, and with that, I haven't ever taken a public stance or position on my sexuality.

My take is this. It's no one's business unless you want it to be.

My house was always open. In the 1990s my mother organised World AIDS Day events in Brighton and Hove, and in hindsight I was clueless about the sexuality or health struggles of anyone who was in and out of our house. People were welcome. Gay or straight. Jewish or not. Perhaps I was raised in an accepting bubble that protected me from the judgement and prejudice of the community around me.

At one point we had a lodger who was converting to Judaism, and we were told she couldn't complete her conversion unless she was living with "a proper family", not a single mother who was pouring her all into raising her child, navigating health challenges, and volunteering for way too many community organisations and charities.

One of my lifelong friends transitioned while at university in Manchester, and I would routinely come home from work or school to find my friend hanging out with my mum, because our home was a place where no one had to sacrifice who they were.

My great-grandmother was a bit of an icon. In a small Jewish community in a seaside town, a lot of people may say that, but there are anecdotes of members of our family travelling abroad, telling strangers they hail from Brighton, and being asked if they know the lady with the silver beehive hair who sat on the bench on The Drive.

This was my great-grandmother.

The tiny woman who loved her family more than anyone, stacked more stuffed toys on her bed than anyone I'd ever known, and lifted her great-grandchildren over the kitchen sink to peer out of the window as the trains passed by, until she was well into her eighties.

Bubbah, as she was known, always greeted you with a smile, and as you ran into her flat you'd be handed a Penguin, a chocolate-covered biscuit that is probably the English version of a Tim Tam. Her dining room, on the left as you walked in, was pristine and reserved for special occasions: Rosh Hashanah meals, Friday night dinners, and more people than you could imagine fitting around a table. That was how we expressed love in our family.

Bubbah had three daughters, all of whom lived nearby, and they loved each other intensely, in all the different ways that word can be read. Sometimes they were the inseparable trio. Sometimes they didn't talk for weeks at a time. But they were there for each other for every marriage and death, every high and low. Until I was sixteen I thought I had the largest family in the world. Countless cousins, as well as honorary uncles and aunts who joined family gatherings just like those we were actually related to. But I don't think we gathered as a single extended family again after we laid Bubbah to rest in 2001. It was at that moment we became three separate families with three leading ladies.

A quarter of a century later, when it was time to gather at the cemetery to say goodbye to my grandmother, the rabbi approached me before I said a few graveside words. He complimented me, celebrating the fact that in the nearly 120 years since my great-grandmother was born, my children, four generations later, were the only ones with two Jewish parents.

I don't say that with judgement or discrimination. It's simply a fact.

I am quite a traditionalist, and I feel a sense of responsibility to my people and my faith. Never did I consider a world in which I would have a non-Jewish partner. Not because Jews are "better" or "chosen", but because, as this book demonstrates, almost every element of my day is touched by Judaism, even down to the way I tie my shoelaces (right, then left). My day-to-day, my normal way of life, isn't truly normal at all. There's nuance, and there's practice, and then it's all layered with my level of practice at the time and how I'm connecting with where I came from.

I love my family. We are one, we came from the same place, and when we do get together it's laughter and memories and joy. With the occasional family argument, which just goes to show how normal we really are after all.

## Chapter 12: The Plot of Land (v0.1)

For over 100 years, our family has gathered around tables in Brighton and Hove. The generations had our hands in many of the organisations that have been and gone. I believe the names of my great-grandparents are on the foundation stones of at least two of the local synagogues.

Today, we met for breakfast in the BNJC, the new Brighton and Hove Jewish Community Centre that recently opened. As I relaxed in the cafe that sits exactly where my school dining room was, on the land where I learned to read Hebrew, there was a hint of a new chapter for Brighton. Never did I imagine a kosher restaurant could serve a Full English alongside shakshuka in Hove.

People were coming and going, some for coffee and pastries, others to chat with friends, all while people in the adjacent shul were preparing for Shabbat, which starts at sundown tonight. This building is beautiful, purpose-built, and has the potential to become the heart and soul of a community. I really hope it thrives. I want to be a supporter of this venture.

All community projects have too many cooks in the kitchen. That's just how we, as active members of a Jewish community, express our love for one another. We sat around the table and remembered the Mark Luck Hall, the stairs on which I tied my shoelaces for the first time, and the countless Shabbat kiddushes the former building had hosted. We shared our views on what we would have done differently if we had led this project. Not out of criticism or critique, but out of love and excitement for this brand-new chapter.

Just like community projects with strong opinions, this family is carrying over 100 years of Brighton baggage that manifests through talking over each other, well-intentioned meddling, taking too many bad photographs, answering questions that weren't asked, laughter, joy, and over-thinking.

For every minute of frustration, there's a warm smile of Jewish continuity from above, as the generations of those who loved us are just happy that we still want to sit down together every time we can.

The shul is full. The restaurant is bustling, compared with the days of struggling to get a quorum for prayer services, and of the school shutting down.

My role in that was nothing more than being an observer. That is why being invited to lead marketing at BNJC was an opportunity I could not turn down.

This Jewish community centre stands on the site of my old school, the place that gave me the foundation to become the Jew I am today.

### Brighton, briefly

Someone is going to mention Middle Street to you, or Bear Road, or Holland Road, entirely certain you know what they mean, and you will nod politely while having no idea. This isn't a Jewish-Brightonian version of Mornington Crescent (any Radio 4 listeners here?), so here is what they are talking about.

Brighton and Hove has been home to Jewish life for a very long time, and the city carries the fingerprints of it.

If you look carefully in Brunswick Square, on the rooftop of 26 Brunswick Terrace, you will see a small dome. In fact, Jew Street was aptly named, as there was a synagogue there in the 1790s.

So, Jewish Brighton is pretty old. Middle Street and Holland Road were Orthodox synagogues that used to be in Brighton and Hove. Bear Road is the Jewish cemetery, and if anyone asks, Cantors used to be the kosher deli on Richardson Road.

### Questions this room raises

**Is a Jewish life a small, joyless life of rules?** No. Here is a myth so quiet that nobody even says it out loud, which makes it the most powerful one of all: that living a Jewish life means living a small, joyless life of rules and religious persecution. That the observant go home to a stringent life of oppression and TV-less boredom. Well, that isn't true. And when it is, it's not because of Judaism.

We go to concerts. We go to the cinema, we go to restaurants, we go on holiday, we watch the same nonsense on television as everyone else and have opinions about it that we will share whether you asked or not.

An Orthodox family that keeps every rule in the book to the letter may also have a favourite takeaway, a group chat full of dark jokes, and strong feelings about a football team. The observance and the ordinary life aren't in tension. There is a balance, and a way to marry the joy of both. They're the same life. Shabbat is a day off from the world, not a life sentence away from it.

I labour this because it matters to how you see the people around you. The man in the black hat is not a monk. The woman covering her hair is not oppressed and joyless. Yes, there are instances of things like this that need to change, and they will change, but anyone who explains these behaviours as being part of being Jewish has misunderstood the gift they have been given.

**Why is there always room for one more at the table?** Because it is normal to bring an unannounced guest to Shabbat, so everyone over-caters and secretly hopes more people turn up than were invited.

## Banked: The Door Was Always Open

*Published on Speaking From Within, 31 December 2025, for your grandmother's first yahrzeit. Kept verbatim. Candidate uses: the dedication, the close of Chapter 1, or the close of Chapter 12.*

When the sun sets in the Jewish calendar tonight, it will be one year. One year since your last smoked salmon sandwich. One year since you sipped a scotch, since we heard your laughter, since we heard you complain.

Your flat stands empty, a precious time capsule for now, a tribute to the hundreds, if not thousands of people that came through your doors for the happiest, and saddest, days of their lives. No matter the occasion, no matter the need, the door was always open, often literally, for the entire community to feel at ease.

The views of Sussex, breathtaking as always, once the cornerstone of our family gatherings, locked in our core memories, will soon be turned over to a new family to share; for a new family to call that magical home their own.

Always a trend-setter, a lot of special people joined you this year. A lot of sadness. A lot to remember. You outlived most of your generation and your memory lives strong. Your entrepreneurial spirit and can-do mindset has been inherited by your great-grandchildren. Your knack for turning any Tuesday into a dinner party for dozens continues in my home.

One year since your heart stopped and your soul returned to so many people you adored, and who adored you too; childhood friends, parents, and partners. For all the joy you shared with those around you, you were no stranger to the pain of loss.

At your stone setting I shared a thought from Pirkei Avot: במקום שאין אנשים, השתדל להיות איש. In a place where there are no people, strive to be that person. You never had to strive to fill a room, you never had to work hard to set an example, it all came naturally to you, and you didn't waste a moment being that person. And just like the stone we set above your grave, the stone I remember you writing yourself when I was in single digits, your memory is a permanent fixture in our lives.

You always loved a celebration so perhaps it's fitting that your first Yahrzeit coincides with New Year's Eve. So whatever it's like up there, and whoever you are with, know how much we miss you, and wish you were here.

*Muriel Elkin-Rose, 1 March 1929 to 12 January 2025*
