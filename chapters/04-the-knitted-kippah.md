# Chapter 4: The Knitted Kippah (v0.1)

*v0.1, polished from dictation, 12 October 2026.*

I went to both a strictly Orthodox school in Manchester and King David, a big Jewish school with children from every kind of home. I may not be the only person who went to both. But I am almost certain I am the only one who ping-ponged between them, back and forth, as my emotional, physical and religious development collided.

At the first, I had to conform. To the rules, and to the stringencies. If you are reading this book to learn about Judaism, it will sound absurd that a boy could be thrown out of school for wearing a knitted kippah. But this was a corner of the community at the far end of the map, and that was the rule.

So I had two. Every afternoon, once I was far enough from the school gates and out of sight of my teachers and classmates, I would take the acceptable one off my head and put on the one I felt like myself in. I don't remember when it started, or even where that kippah came from.

I was not only hiding from a rule. Some of those teachers, had they caught me, would have had no trouble tearing into me with words. None of them ever laid a hand on me. But in that place, the fear that one might was always in the room.

I am still not sure whether I should be proud that this was the height of my teenage rebellion, or sad that I lived in a world where it counted as one.

And then I found Bnei Akiva. Religious Zionism made sense to me in a way nothing had before: Jewish continuity, Jewish pride, a people deciding its own future, and none of it built out of fences. By my teens I was one of a handful of Shabbat-observant kids in an enormous school. But I had found a Judaism that let me go to concerts and watch films without feeling ashamed.

I don't remember exactly when I first went. I remember what it did. I stopped being the frightened boy who half expected a thunderbolt from the sky if he mispronounced a blessing, and I started to become a leader.

For a number of years, that building was the happiest place in my life. Nobody asked me to pore over the Talmud for four hours a day or recite the oral law from memory. Instead, Judaism came alive. It tied our ancient customs to the world outside the window, and it made me proud of who I was, in the years before Zionism became a word people used as an insult. Questions were welcome. My Judaism felt intellectually honest, and worth something. Not stuffy, and not restrictive.

It gave me highs and lows. Friendships that are still strong today, and moments of shock and loss that bonded us even tighter.

But here is what has never left me. To this day, when I stand in any synagogue in the world on a Friday night and the congregation begins Kabbalat Shabbat, the songs that welcome Shabbat in, I am back in the octagonal shul at Bnei Akiva in Salford, where I fell in love with those tunes and nobody judged how I sang them.

Floor-to-ceiling windows towered over us. At night they held the stars. At morning services, the sun poured in. In that room our voices and our prayers became one, whatever our politics, whichever school we went to, man or woman.

It was the first room where I saw women ask to dance with the Torah on Simchat Torah, and saw the answer come back yes.

It was the room that taught teenagers to love who they were, not run from it.

Both corners of Jewish Manchester were doing what they believed was right, building community and protecting Jewish continuity. One did it with the fear of banishment. The other did it with song, and a sense of belonging.

I can't point to one moment when Israel became the plan. It came in slowly, like a tide. Between Yad Achim, the anthem of our movement, and Hatikvah, the anthem of our nation, I sang my way into a sense of purpose and pride.

One Yom Ha'atzmaut, Israel's Independence Day, our school hall, big enough to seat a thousand students for assembly, was turned into a papier-mâché map of Israel.

We were split into groups of four, and each group built a piece of the land. It felt fitting. We had grown up on stories of coins dropped into blue JNF boxes and sent off to buy the land, parcel by parcel, from Arab landowners willing to sell.

My group got the Golan Heights. Not long before, I had hiked the Banias and eaten shawarma in Katzrin. Now I was shaping those same hills out of newspaper and paste.

None of it came only from books. Bnot sherut, young Israeli women doing national service, came to our school as emissaries, and they never missed a beat. They made us fall in love with every corner of the land.

## Questions this room raises

**Is "Zionist" a bad word?** No, though the last few years have done a number on it. Underneath the noise it means the belief that the Jewish people, like other peoples, have a right to a home. It comes in a dozen flavours that argue with each other endlessly, and for most Jews it is not a hot political stance. It is woven in.

**Where does Zionism fit?** It is not a denomination, but you cannot pull it out of the religion. Open a prayer book and it points to Jerusalem. Most festivals and most lifecycle moments reach toward Zion somewhere in the words. The longing is as old as the tradition. Modern Zionism grew out of the same moment as the denominations: when the nations of Europe were waking up to self-government, Jews asked the same question of themselves. That is why a Reform Jew and an Orthodox Jew who agree on almost nothing else will be seen celebrating Israel at the same event.

Also true: historians document expulsions by Jewish forces too, Lydda and Ramle among them. Say both.
- **Before 1948.** Haj Amin al-Husseini met Hitler in November 1941 and worked for the Nazi regime. True and usable.
- **1967.** Israel launched a pre-emptive strike on 5 June, after Egypt expelled UN peacekeepers, closed the Straits of Tiran and massed troops on the border. Name the first strike and why it was pre-emptive in the same sentence. Jordan, in a defence pact with Egypt, joined the fighting, and that is how the West Bank and East Jerusalem changed hands. Deaths: Israel fewer than 1,000; the Arab states more than 15,000. Ammunition Hill: 36 Israeli and 71 Jordanian soldiers killed. Dayan hesitated to take the Old City. The West Bank answer is about Jordan's choice to fight, not Gaza.
- **Carter and Gaza.** Defensible version, from the State Department record: Israel captured Gaza from Egypt in 1967. Camp David returned Sinai to Egypt and folded Gaza into a plan for Palestinian self-rule alongside the West Bank, which never happened, so Gaza stayed with Israel. When Sadat later pushed to deal with Gaza separately, Carter objected that this was not what was agreed at Camp David. Say Carter kept Gaza tied to the West Bank; don't say he forced it on Israel.
- **Sinai to Gaza.** Some families evacuated from Yamit in 1982 did resettle in Gush Katif. That was 23 years before 2005, not fifty.
- **Egypt's border.** Egypt kept Rafah largely closed. Usable.
- **Electricity.** Not free. Israel sells it; the bill is deducted from tax revenue Israel collects for the Palestinian Authority. Drop this point.

Zionism is simple, and it isn't evil. It is the belief that the Jewish people have the right to determine their own future in their ancestral homeland. It is an old prayer, said in our liturgy, in our daily prayers, in the words we offer to mourners. It is older than the British Mandate, older than the Ottomans, older than every empire that ever expelled us from the land.

So here is my question. If Zionism is colonialism, where is the mother country? Where were the spoils sent?

[Draft for your edit.] Here is the difference. A settler arrives somewhere new. Jews came back somewhere ancient. Our prayers face it. Our festivals follow its harvests. Our prayers for rain follow its seasons, even when we are saying them in Manchester in October. Nobody writes three thousand years of longing for a place they have never been.

And the picture of white Europeans planting flags falls apart the moment you meet Israelis. Around half of Israel's Jews trace their families to Baghdad, Casablanca, Sana'a, Aleppo and Tehran. Many of their grandparents did not arrive as colonists. They arrived as refugees, pushed out of the Arab world after 1948, with nowhere else that would take them.
