# Chapter 1: The Break-Fast (v0.2)

*Recovered draft v0.2 (19 July 2026), placed verbatim. Needs a pass: em dashes out, and the "how this book works" section still describes the old calendar structure.*

The first time — and, so far, the only time — I have ever climbed into a fire engine, it was parked outside my grandmother's art deco flat.

She lived on the sixth floor, with views of the Sussex Downs on one side and the seafront stretching for miles on the other. The wallpaper was gold and velvet stripe, and the flat was a constant of my life until the day she died: always open, whether for a cup of tea, a glass of scotch in front of the evening news, or one of the occasions when trestle tables filled the room and the room somehow agreed to hold them.

So there was nothing strange about the fact that on this particular night, fifty or so of us — aunts, uncles, cousins, friends, neighbours from the building, many of them Jewish too — were squeezed around her table at the end of Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, for the meal that breaks the fast. There was also nothing strange, at least to us, about the fact that only a handful of the fifty had actually fasted, and fewer still had been anywhere near a synagogue. The meal was not a reward for observance. The meal was the observance. Nobody at that table would have put it that way, and nobody needed to.

That was when the fire alarm went off.

It became clear quite quickly that this was not a drill. Fifty of us left the table and filed down the stairs — hundreds of them, it felt like, six storeys' worth — and out into a night already washed in red flashing lights.

My mother and my uncle, without discussion, went back in. They took it upon themselves to go door to door through the building, helping the elderly and the infirm down those same stairs. I should mention that my mother had, at this point, not eaten or drunk anything for twenty-five hours. I was upset about this at the time — not the fasting, the going back in — and registered my protest in the way children do, which is to say I was ignored. My cousins and I were left in the care of the firemen managing the scene, one of whom lifted me into the cab of the engine, which is how I came to make the climb I have never repeated since.

My grandmother, for her part, also had priorities. Before evacuating, she went to the safe and collected her jewellery. Then she put on a fur coat, to keep warm, and came down all six floors dressed for the occasion.

The fire, it turned out, never spread beyond a single flat. Hours later we climbed back up, took our places, and resumed. My mother, who had spent the evening of the holiest day of the year carrying her neighbours' arms down a stairwell, finally broke her twenty-five-hour fast on an apple.

That was my grandmother's flat, and this is my family.

My grandmother described herself, without any sense of contradiction, as a Jewish atheist. She helped fundraise and establish the Reform synagogue in Brighton — and then left the congregation because it wasn't religious enough. If that sentence doesn't make sense to you, good. You are exactly the reader I wrote this book for, and by the end of it, it will make perfect sense, and you will have met a hundred people like her.

We were, in those years, chicken soup on Friday night Jews. Roast chicken and potatoes Jews. Chopped liver Jews. My great-grandmother's chicken soup recipe is still made in our family's homes, generations on, which as far as I can tell makes it the most successfully transmitted piece of Jewish law in our entire lineage.

My parents divorced when I was five or six, and — in the way these things sometimes go — both became far more observant afterwards. I spent my formative years in an ultra-Orthodox community in Manchester, black hat and side curls, being taught a Judaism that you did because you did it, not because you understood it. When my teenage rebellion arrived, it took the form you would least expect: I became a modern Orthodox Jew. I kept the faith, kept kosher, kept Shabbat — and, gasp, watched films and listened to the radio. It was not until my sixteenth birthday that a television entered my house, and only then because my grandmother had inspected the situation and formally ruled me a deprived child.

Manchester was so Jewish that I did not make my first non-Jewish friend until after I had left — working in a call centre in Jerusalem. I want you to sit with the geography of that sentence for a moment. I had to move to the Middle East to meet a gentile.

Fast forward to my forties: a divorced father of three, carrying a hundred and one questions about faith and practice and sexuality and prayer and tradition, and none of the answers I had been raised on. I had the enormous luck of finding a rabbi who got all of it — who could tell the difference between the "sacred" and the actually sacred, who approached religion with logic and without dismissing science or history, and who, not to overstate it, saved my relationship with our faith.

Like the generations before me, I have spent my life inside Jewish communities — sometimes as part of their infrastructure, sometimes just by opening my own front door and building the village I wanted around me. And along the way I have learned something from every flavour of Jew there is.

Here is what all of it taught me, and here is the premise of this book.

There is no one way to Jew. My mother's way, that night, was the stairwell. My grandmother's way was the fur coat — and she would want you to know the fur coat was also correct. The black hats of Manchester have their way; the Reform congregation my grandmother founded and then flounced out of has theirs; and none of them is the fake version of somebody else's real thing. Nobody is doing this wrong on purpose. Judaism has been evolving for three thousand years, and every Jew you will ever meet is standing somewhere on that moving map, doing their honest best with the coordinates they were given.

I didn't always believe this. For years I was sure there was exactly one right way to be a Jew, and that it came with a black hat, which I happened to be wearing. It took me most of my life, and every room in this book, to unlearn it.

And I know what it is like to stand outside the glass, because I once stood on the other side of it. I once knew as little about you as you may know about us. So let me say the thing this whole book exists to say: you don't need to be afraid to be yourself around us. You are allowed to ask. If you are worried a question might be offensive, the worry itself is usually proof that it's fine. We are people — with our quirks, our arguments, our recipes that outlive our beliefs — and most of us, honestly, are quite a nice bunch anyway.

A quick word about how this book works, and then we'll get going.

The Jewish year does not run on your calendar. It runs on the moon, which is why the holidays seem to wander around your scheduling app like they've lost their keys — so rather than marching from January to December, we'll walk the year the way we actually live it, starting in September, where it begins. If you only need to know why half your office is out in October, there's a cheat sheet for that; the rest of you can take the scenic route with me.

Between the parts of this book you'll find pages of rapid-fire questions — the ones people are usually too embarrassed to ask out loud, answered the way I'd answer a text from a friend. Can you invite us to your Christmas party? (Yes. We might even come.) Do all rabbis have beards? (No. Stay tuned.) Nothing asked in good faith is off the table; that is roughly the founding principle of my entire life.

And in the practical chapters — food, Shabbat, weddings, mourning — I'll finish with the part you can act on: what to bring, what to say, what never to say in a house of mourning. Whoever you are — colleague, friend, neighbour, in-law-to-be, or a Jew reading over someone's shoulder to check whether I got it right — you're who I wrote this for.

How you Jew is yours. How you come to know us — that part, I can help with. Come on up. The kettle's on, the scotch is open, and if the fire alarm goes off, you'll want to see what my grandmother wears down the stairs.

## Questions this room raises

**Why are there so many kinds of Jew?** Because Judaism is the expression of what it is to be part of the Jewish ethnic group. It's the expression of walking through the desert for forty years and ending up in the Promised Land, only to be chased out and have to roam around the diaspora. To be persecuted in Europe, to be told to go back to where you came from, only to be told by the world to leave again once you get there.

Much like many aspects of life, development, sexuality and intellect, faith sits on a spectrum, and everyone belongs. And right or left of that spectrum isn't better or worse than anyone else's way of doing things. Part of the reason for all these different Jewish expressions comes from a period called the Haskalah, or Enlightenment. In the 1770s there was tremendous change going on in the world. Empires were falling, monarchies were collapsing, and power was shifting to the people. Jews were leaving the ghettos, and at that intersection with society, they had to find an identity. As a result, Jewish people found ways to live their lives as English, Czech, French, Polish, Russian, Moroccan, American, Spanish and Portuguese people who were also Jews, rather than being Jews who happened to live in those places. So the movements we see today, Orthodox, Reform, Conservative, Progressive, Chabad and Zionist, all have their roots in different responses to the same historical context. Each of these sects had intellectuals and philosophers spreading their own How to Jew manuscripts, rewriting the rules for a new time, and traditions started forming.

There is an old joke about a Jew rescued from a desert island who is asked why he built two synagogues. One is the one he goes to. The other is the one he would never set foot in.

**Give me the short version.** Orthodox Jews keep traditional practice closely and treat Jewish law as binding. Reform keeps the tradition but reads it more flexibly for modern life. Conservative, or Masorti, sits between: traditional in practice, open in interpretation.

**Are Progressive and Reform, or Conservative and Masorti, different things?** Mostly the same things in different coats. Reform in America is close to Progressive in Britain, where Reform and Liberal Judaism have joined. Conservative in America is Masorti here.

**Is Chabad a fourth movement?** No. Chabad is a Hasidic group, and Hasidism is a stream within Orthodoxy. They are known for warmth, outreach, and turning up anywhere in the world to run a Shabbat dinner.
