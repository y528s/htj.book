# Chapter 8: The Little Hat on My Head (v0.1)

*Recovered draft v0.3 (19 July 2026), originally titled "What Exactly Are You?", placed verbatim. Needs a pass: em dashes out; the Malaysia and Green Party beat is dated to July 2026; the dating line in the kinship paragraph is a holdover from the three-reader frame.*

The morning after a tornado swept through my neighbourhood in suburban Kansas, I stepped onto the patio and found a sixty-foot tree lying at my feet. Some jobs are not DIY jobs. I hired a professional.

He showed up a few days later — early forties, like me, built solid from a career outdoors, an American flag tattooed on his forearm, the kind of man who shakes your hand and means it. Over the three days he worked in my garden I learned that he volunteered at his church; that he had adopted his special-needs niece when his sister could no longer care for her; that he loved his work because it let him help people on the worst day of their week. By any honest measure, he was an all-American mensch. You'll learn that word later in this book. He earned it.

It is very obvious that mine is a Jewish home. I wear a kippah — I always have, wherever I've been on the winding road of my own observance. There are mezuzot on every doorpost, Israeli radio on the smart speaker, and a name on the invoice, Yosef Silver, that is a giveaway to anyone not trying to sneak an extra "u" between the "o" and the "s."

And he was curious — warmly, unashamedly curious, which as you'll discover is my favourite quality in a person. He asked about the little hat on my head, and whether all Jewish people wear them all the time. He asked about the stream of friends and kids and neighbours who kept nipping in and out of the house unannounced while he worked. He overheard phone calls about Shabbat potlucks and asked about those too. I answered everything, happily. He told me about the richness his church gave his life, and how good it was to see people who actually knew their neighbours' names. For three days we schlepped branches to the woodchipper together, me with my tea, him with his Big Gulp. Then the work was done.

We stood by his truck. He shook my hand and said he'd come back any time I needed. We said we should grab a beer. And then, with the same warmth he'd carried all three days, with the easy smile of a man who had decided he liked me, he said:

"I don't know why guys don't like you Jews. You look white, anyway."

I didn't say anything. I just stood there while it washed over me, and he got in his truck and drove away.

We never got that beer.

I want to be fair to him, because he was, in every other moment, a good man — and because I suspect he has no idea that sentence was anything other than a compliment. He meant it as acceptance. And the reason a kind man can say something like that is not malice. It's that he was working from the wrong map.

Here is the map he had, because it's the map almost everyone has. There are three files a person can go in: *religion* — a thing you believe; *ethnicity* — a culture you come from; *race* — a thing you are. Pick one. He'd spent three days trying to work out which file Jews belong in. The potlucks said religion. The food and the open door said culture. And the last sentence, the one he didn't know he was answering, said race.

Let me save you the three days. The answer is yes.

To show you what I mean, I have to tell you where I come from — and then about the worst Sunday morning of my teenage years.

From a young age, I was told the tale: my family fled the Spanish Inquisition, made it to Holland, and from there entered England in the era of Cromwell, when the country let the Jews back in after nearly four hundred years of official absence. And for all the generations since, we have been thoroughly, cartoonishly English — the cups of tea, the strong opinions about whether cream or jam goes on the scone first. But I was raised knowing the distinction cold: I am English because of where I am. What I *am* travelled here from somewhere else.

Years later — before I understood how unwise it is to hand your genetic code to a company that might someday sell it — I did one of those at-home DNA tests, curious to see whether the Cromwellian family legend held up. The results came back and settled the question with almost comic finality: to within a rounding error, the test said one thing. Jewish, with the remainder pointing at the hills around the Levant. Three and a half centuries in England, and England had contributed the accent, the tea, and nothing you could find in a cheek swab. Whatever my family had been doing since the Inquisition, marrying out was not it. My Jewish identity, it turns out, was nature all the way down — and then nurture built on top of it.

That's the "race" answer, and I give it to you without flinching, because history has never flinched about it either. When the world has come for us — Spain in the 1490s, Germany in the 1940s — it never once asked what we believed. The Inquisition burned converts to Christianity on suspicion of secret Jewishness; the Nuremberg laws counted grandparents, not prayers. The men with the clipboards always knew what the census forms still can't decide: this thing is in the blood, whether or not it's in the heart.

But blood is only half of what I mean when I say there's no way out. For the other half, I have to take you to Sunday Club.

As a teenager, I went to a youth group on Sunday mornings — aptly named Sunday Club — that existed to be a safe room for the conversations the Orthodox world usually files under *taboo*. What would happen if Israel ceased to exist. What Judaism actually says about sex before marriage. Nothing was off the table, which is precisely why we came. I was, at the time, the kind of teenager who kept two kippahs — one for yeshiva, and a different one for being out in the world — which tells you most of what you need to know about being sixteen inside a community that is watching.

One Sunday, our youth leader — today a well-known rabbi and author — posed this: *What would you do if you found out tomorrow morning that there'd been a mistake, and you weren't actually Jewish after all?*

The answers went around the room. Some said they'd convert, obviously — straight to the rabbinical court, restore the paperwork. Others said they'd convert *after* a cheeseburger, on the once-in-a-lifetime technicality. Everyone laughed.

And I sat there spiralling like Truman when he sails his boat into the painted sky, because I couldn't find the version of me that survived the question. Take away *Jewish*, and what happened to Shabbat — the day that stitched my week back together, the table, the songs, the long unhurried walk? What happened to the connection — the people who knew me without explanation, the homes I could walk into unannounced, the certainty that in any city on earth there was a door that would open for me? Even the way I saw the world — the passion for what was right and the fury at what wasn't — I had learned that somewhere, and the somewhere was this. Everything I'd been taught from my youngest days, everything I have questioned since — and I have questioned all of it, daily, up to and including the morning I write these words — where would the questions even live, if the thing I was questioning was never mine? Because that's what I understood, sitting in that room: questioning it was never the same as leaving it. The arguing was the belonging. It wasn't like being asked to change religions. It was like being told the set wasn't real.

That spiral is the honest answer to what Judaism is. Because yes — it is prayer and practice. Rituals and recipes. But underneath all of it, it's a people you belong to, with everything belonging brings: the network that has caught me in every city I've ever lived in, and the prying eyes that made a teenager keep a second kippah in his pocket. Both. The community that will drive you mad is the same one that will show up, uninvited and fully catered, on the worst day of your life. You don't believe your way into that, and you can't disbelieve your way out of it.

And Jewish law, for once, agrees with the lived experience completely: you cannot resign. Stop practicing — still a Jew. Marry out — still a Jew. Convert to something else entirely, announce it in the newspaper — the tradition will shrug and keep your seat. On this one point, grimly, the rabbis and the persecutors have always held the same position. Nobody gets to leave the tribe. It's not a membership. It's kinship — which, if you happen to be dating one of us, is the most useful thing this book will tell you: you are not taking on someone's beliefs, you are joining someone's people, and the family dinner will always matter more than the theology conversation.

Here is the one feature that breaks every category you'd try to put us in: the door does open — inward. A convert arrives with none of my Levantine cheek-swab and becomes fully, completely, irrevocably a Jew. Not an associate member. Not a Jew-with-an-asterisk. In, forever — no-exit clause included; the door swings one way for everybody. We are, as far as I can tell, the only "race" in human history that issues naturalization papers. King David's own great-grandmother was a convert named Ruth. We put her book in the Bible and read it standing up.

A religion that doesn't require belief — you met my grandmother, the synagogue-founding atheist; a friend of mine once put it perfectly: Christianity is for the followers of Christ, but Judaism is how Jews express who we already are, which is why there's no pope, no leader to follow, and a thousand ways to do it. An ethnicity, in a dozen regional flavours that get their own chapter. And a race with an entrance and no exit. Hold all three at once and you finally have the right map.

Now you can hear what the man by the truck actually said. Not *I accept you* — *I've cleared you*. You pass. And passing is not belonging; it's a conditional visa, revocable at a glance — and one my family has held before. We passed in Spain, right up until we didn't. Jews were barred from the clubs and quotas and neighbourhoods of "white" England and America within living memory; the whiteness on offer now is recent, partial, and — every Jew quietly knows — returnable.

I don't even have to reach back for proof. In the very week I write this sentence, the prime minister of Malaysia announced that any Israeli found in his country will be deported on sight. In London, the leader of the Green Party — himself Jewish — has backed a public call for the government to keep records of British nationals who served in Israel's army, with extra screening at the border; its authors insist it has nothing to do with Jews, while the Board of Deputies calls it a way of marking people out for intimidation. Wherever you stand on any of it, notice the shape: lists, borders, papers. The oldest suspicions never die. They just update their vocabulary.

Besides, the premise fails on contact: walk into any synagogue in Jerusalem and try to sort the room by your racial categories. You'll give up by the second row.

He looked at me carefully for three days and decided I was acceptable. What I'd thought we were doing, over the branches and the tea and the Big Gulps, was becoming known.

The tree, in the end, became firewood. It burns in my firepit on Saturday nights while we make Havdalah, the little ceremony that ends Shabbat — the tornado that started all of this now keeping my tribe warm in the garden.

He asked every question right, that man, for three days — until the one he didn't know he was asking. This chapter exists so that you never have to answer it the way he did. You now know what we are. All of it. Yes.

## Questions this room raises

**Are Jews a race, a religion or an ethnicity?** All three at once, which is why the question ties people in knots. A religion you can practise, an ethnicity you are born into, a people you belong to. There is an entrance, conversion, but no real exit, which is why a proud atheist can still be unquestionably Jewish.

**Can you be half-Jewish?** The movements answer differently. Some trace it through the mother, some through either parent. It is a live debate.

**Can you stop being Jewish?** Not really. There's a door in. There isn't really a door out.

**Could I become Jewish?** Yes. Conversion is a serious, guided process. Once you are in, you are fully in, forever. And nobody has ever tried to convert you, because Judaism doesn't go looking for recruits. The first stop is a conversation with a rabbi.
