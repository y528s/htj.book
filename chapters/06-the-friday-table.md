# Chapter 6: The Friday Table (v0.1)

Friday night is the highlight of my week. Every single day of the week leads here. Whether it's a dinner for thirty-five squeezed into a dining room that comfortably seats twelve, or a quiet night at home alone while my kids are at their mother's house, when I lift that cup for Kiddush on Friday night and the blessing over the wine is sung or said, everything melts away.

Shabbat is incredibly important to me, and the Friday night service at synagogue is one of my favourites, but I rarely manage to attend. Many would look at my lax approach to the rules of the Sabbath and have a solid argument that I no longer practise in a way that would be considered Orthodox.

Perhaps this is why I struggle with labels and boxes in Judaism. And perhaps this is the first time I'm publicly sharing my stance on Shabbat and religious practice.

A typical Friday now looks something like this. Texts fly between friends, and while we have a little more structure, and usually know earlier in the week who will dine together and that the grocery shopping has been done, it's rare that I know the exact makeup of my Friday night table until we sit down at it. The joy of the front door opening because someone has walked past and seen us at the table, or teens popping by for, or with, my own kids, is just part of the fabric of Jewish life.

No, I don't always make chicken soup, or roast chicken and roast potatoes, and no, I don't always have guests. But I never touch a computer on Shabbat, and I never work. Red lines.

I prefer to set the lights beforehand and make sure the oven is left on a timer. But at this stage of owning my religious practice, and making sure it's meaningful, I may jump on a train or into a car if it means a real connection or social enrichment will come of it. And this is how I ended up at Pride Shabbat in 2025. There was a chance to attend a Shabbat service and dinner with a friend and strengthen my sense of Jewish belonging, even if it meant jumping on the Tube.

When you bring a room together with food and wine, people and song, and you are all there, screen-free, that is where the magic happens. I've sat in countless countries and met strangers who became friends for the night, or friends for life.

When living in Israel, our Friday morning bagel ritual usually ended once the hangover started to subside and someone decided they'd host dinner for our crew that weekend. We'd all make or bake something and show up with a bottle too. There would be last-minute calls to pick up a dessert or to come and help roll meatballs before the deadline of sunset was upon us.

A good friend and I had an unwritten rule. We knew each of us was likely still reachable by telephone a little after sunset: "Once we started Shabbat, we followed the rules, but until then we were flexible." This modern approach to religious life isn't uncommon, but in the Manchester I grew up in it would have been grounds for excommunication. In my Israel days and my adult life, it's become a way to keep my faith and religion alive, and to give me friends to connect with.

One of my most memorable Shabbat dinners took place once I lived in Kansas. I had a phase of being a somewhat known kosher food blogger, and this led me to connect with a British Indian author, Simon Majumdar, who was writing a book called Fed, White, and Blue, in which he sought to cook and dine with as many faces of America as he could find. Not only did Simon come to my house on Friday to cook with me, and share a smoked turkey korma recipe adapted from his family secrets for the rules of a kosher kitchen, but he spent the Shabbat dinner with us too, and witnessed the song and community first-hand. He was also impressed that my wife was able to make dairy-free desserts, following our meat meal, that didn't sacrifice on taste.

And my children were wildly impressed that the man they had seen as a judge on their favourite foodie TV shows was sitting in their home, discussing culinary adventures from barbecue to the quality of kosher vermouth.

## Questions this room raises

**What actually is Shabbat?** A full day of stopping, in honour of the day God rested after making the world. Once a week the world is told to wait.

**Shabbat or Shabbos?** Both. Same word, two pronunciations. Which one someone uses tells you where they are from more than what they believe.

**Why no electricity?** Shabbat pauses acts of making, and completing a circuit is treated as one. So lights, ovens and switches are set before sundown and left alone. It looks like a rule about electricity. It is really a rule about not building things on your day off.

**Why does the lift stop at every floor on a Saturday?** Shabbat mode, so a person can ride without pressing a button. A piece of engineering built entirely so someone can have a day off from buttons.

**Why did a Jewish friend ask me to turn on a light?** Because they can't and you can, and asking is allowed where doing it is not. They may hint: "it's dark in here". You are free to say yes or no.

**What is a Shabbos goy, and is it offensive?** A non-Jew who helps with what a Jew can't do on Shabbat. The term is old and usually affectionate. Whether it lands badly is all tone and relationship.

**Do Jewish people use money on Shabbat?** The observant don't. No cash, no cards, no signing a bill. Paid dinners are paid in advance.

**Can someone ride in a car on Shabbat?** For the strictly observant, no. Others draw the line differently. Two people, same faith, different answer.

**Why won't someone praying answer me?** In some prayers you don't break off partway. Wait a minute. They will be with you.

**What is havdalah?** The short ceremony that ends Shabbat, with wine, spices and a plaited candle, once three stars are out. That is why people stand outside on a Saturday night looking up.

**Why an urn and a hotplate?** Because you can't cook once Shabbat starts. Hot water and Friday's food are kept going without anyone touching a dial.

**What is a Shabbat lamp?** A lamp with a sliding cover, so you can make a room dark or light without switching anything.

**What is an eruv?** On Shabbat you don't carry between private and public space, which sounds minor until it means you can't push a pram to synagogue. An eruv is a boundary, usually walls and thin wire on poles, that turns an area into one shared space where carrying is allowed.
