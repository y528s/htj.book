# Chapter 5: The Rock in the Sea (v0.1)

Assembled from dictation. Square brackets mark gaps to fill.

There is a folded piece of paper in my ID wallet. It has been there for [twenty] years. It has gone soft at the creases, and it has moved with me from Jerusalem to Kansas to Brighton and back again. I have never shown anyone what it says. I am not going to start here.

What I can tell you is the night I wrote it.

## Walking

I walk everywhere. Jerusalem rewards it. It is a city of the old and the new, the coffee and the sakhlav, the parks and the stone, the religious and the secular, the Jew and the Arab. And the laughter and the sadness. Children play and laugh on the swings, and across the road a small plaque names the people who were killed at that bus stop years before. Nobody stops to read it, and nobody forgets it. The swings keep going.

That is what we mean when we say Am Yisrael Chai. The people of Israel live. Not survive. Live.

I first climbed Ammunition Hill as a schoolboy, and I have been back several times since. It sits in the middle of Jerusalem now, with a light rail stop at its foot. In 1967 it was a Jordanian army post, and on 6 June, 36 Israeli paratroopers and 71 Jordanian soldiers died fighting through its trenches. Olive trees stand on the hill, one for each Israeli soldier who fell in the battle for Jerusalem. Commuters step off the tram at the bottom on their way to work.

I tell myself I walk because I love the city, and I do. But I also do not get on buses. I arrived in the years after the bombings of the Second Intifada, and something in me decided early that buses were for other people. Forty-five minutes on foot is nothing. I barely leave Jerusalem. When I do, a shirut to Tel Aviv or a Shabbat with friends in the Gush, there is a knot I have to undo before I can get in the car.

Whenever I took a bus, I did it with caution. It was a choice between walking across town at the crack of dawn or rolling the dice on an evolving PTSD I wasn't fully aware of. A sleepless twenty-one-year-old would sometimes roll those dice.

I woke up on a thin foam mattress on the cold tiled floor of a friend's flat in Sha'arei Chesed, on a cold Jerusalem morning, and it hit me. I had just taken my first one-way flight. I lived in Israel now.

It wasn't my first time there. Not even close. My mother scraped together her pennies to take me when I was eight, and a week into our fortnight in the Holy Land I caught dysentery. After that I went back more times than I can count. At first it was thanks to the people who ran my high school, who made sure no student ever missed a trip for lack of money. Later I paid my own way, with evening shifts in a shop and, eventually, by skiving school to work as a graphic designer for a local magazine. Whenever I could, I nipped over for a summer or a visit.

So deciding to move wasn't hard.

My relationship with Israel didn't begin when I started forming opinions, any more than the Jewish people's relationship with the land began with a UN vote in 1947. It was simply there, the way it is for every Jew who prays.

People thinking about aliyah often ask me how my mother took the news. Was she supportive? Did I have to make my case?

Honestly, she always knew. Mothers know things, even when we think we have secrets. The good ones drop hints, make sure you know you are loved whatever you choose, and let you become the adult you are meant to be. Mine did all three.

I came through the front door one Friday afternoon. Ruth, who cleaned for us, was hoovering for Shabbat. Ruth was family. She came from a rough part of town, and when our house was burgled when I was twelve, it was Ruth who worked her network and got our things back, not the police.

I shouted up the stairs. "Hi Mum! I'm moving to Israel in January!"

My mother shouted back down. "That's great. When?"

Ruth had a small conniption. "What the hell just happened here? Do you think I'm going to let you move to the Middle East?"

Ruth went on cleaning for my mother for another eighteen months. Then my mother fulfilled the dream she had carried since she was a teenager herself, and took the first one-way flight of her life too.

My roommate owned a bar called Sugar Hill, and it became our social hub.

A lot of us worked American hours, which in Jerusalem start in the middle of the afternoon. So by day we sat at the bar with reruns of It's Always Sunny in Philadelphia on the screen, planning our lives. Hunting for flats. Hunting for jobs. Navigating a Jerusalem dating scene so small and tangled that everyone knew everyone's business.

Two of my friends met their spouses at that bar. Coy first kisses, relationships that lasted forever, and everything in between started there. I met a girlfriend there, and twenty years later we are still friends. One evening an acquaintance took a pregnancy test in the middle of a bartending shift, and found out right there.

Jerusalem has a way of making a family out of you. We were rooted in ritual, just not the religious kind. Friday morning bagels. Thursday guys' nights. And knowing the door at Sugar Hill was always open. It was our Wild West saloon in the heart of the Holy City.

The Sugar Hill days led to a summer of weddings. I think the summer I married, I was the eighth of our social circle, one of the final weddings of the season, and we had spent the summer sharing notes on caterers, photographers, venues and the like.

I barely left Jerusalem, except for work. For three of my years there, work was in Lod. Back then, Lod was an industrial park close enough to the airport to have a missile strike protocol, and far enough away that it wasn't cool. At first I took the bus from Baka into town, where a friend drove a few of us down. Over time one colleague became my roommate and another my neighbour, and the commute got easier. Mostly I slept in the back seat.

The office was split in two. On one side were the Anglos, mostly Orthodox, speaking English all day. On the other were the Israelis, mostly secular, who had served in the army together and spoke Hebrew. Our two CEOs had been friends all their lives and had served together in the Entebbe raid, the 1976 rescue in which Yoni Netanyahu was killed leading the assault.

For these three years I gained another moniker. In this circle I was nicknamed Mo, because two of my colleagues from the UK had tried to nickname me Mohammad Yousef and I wasn't having it. So this was the happy compromise.

## The rock

My friends are playing a gig in Tel Aviv. They have asked me a dozen times. A dozen times I have found a reason not to go. This time I go.

These days I write on trains instead of looking out of them. I am on one now. Back then, phones were for texting, and a shirut ride was for watching the world go by. Every time I travelled between the country's two great cities, the road held me. The hills I had read about all my life were right there through the glass. Yes, it is beautiful. But it is more than scenery. This is where Jewish history, ancient and modern, comes alive.

At the break, a friend and I walk down to the beach. We roll a joint and climb onto a rock with the water moving around us. [The band behind us, the sound of the sea, what the night smells like.]

We laugh. Then we talk about how lucky we are. Good friends, good jobs, a whole network of people who feel like family. And more than that: we are living in the Jewish land, our homeland, the place so many people fought and died to give us. It is [summer 2005 or 2006], after the Intifada and before the next war. For a moment it feels like the best time in history to be young and Jewish and in Israel.

When I get home that night I write myself a note, so I will never forget that rock. I fold it and put it in my wallet.

## The bus

Israelis are brutally honest. A stranger on a bus will tell you your hair needs cutting and, in the same sentence, invite you for Friday night dinner. And mean it. In Israel, everyone is family, whether you asked to be or not.

That honesty is the gift and the bill.

I am late for work. On any other morning I would walk. This morning I do not have forty-five minutes. So I roll the dice. I run for a bus. The light turns red as I cross. I make it, breathless and proud. Eight seconds later the bus stops. There is a knock on the door. A policeman wants a word, and a fine, for the way I crossed the road.

I have always believed that if you get run over jaywalking, you should have looked. I did not need the State of Israel to teach me. I stand on the pavement holding a ticket, watching the bus leave without me. The one morning I beat the fear, I get fined for it. I understand that I am tired.

## Moving day

By then I was married, with a child.

On moving day the truck parks outside, and a neighbour does not like where. He leans out and shouts that if it is not moved, he will turn his hose on my boxes. [His exact words in Hebrew, if you remember them.]

Something in me snaps. I shout back, fast and furious, in perfect Hebrew. This, I tell him, is exactly why I am leaving the country.

It is the most Israeli I have ever been. I became one on the way out of the door.

## What I could not see then

Perhaps I was simply immature. Perhaps therapy would have fixed it. Or perhaps I was always meant to live abroad, fly an Israeli flag outside my house, and give my energy to Jewish life in the diaspora rather than being swept up in the ordinary life of an ordinary Israeli. In Israel, you do not have to fight to be Jewish. The shops close for Shabbat. The money is the shekel. The language is Hebrew, brought back from the dead. You can just be a tired man with a tax bill. I have always thrived somewhere small, where I can have a say.

Years later, a therapist asks me why I always need to see the exit in a restaurant, and why I avoid cinemas. The word she uses is PTSD. I think about the buses I would not take, the knot before every shirut, and the rock. That night was not only joy. It was courage I did not know I was using.

## The kept bedroom

Every prayer I say faces Jerusalem. Every wedding, every Seder, every festival reaches toward Zion somewhere in the words. Name almost any year in Israeli history and I can tell you what happened. 1956? Suez. Before you finish the question.

For two thousand years we were told to go home. Now that we are home, we are told to leave.

Israel is the parent who keeps your bedroom exactly as you left it, after university, after the wedding, after the move abroad. Not because they expect you back tomorrow. Because they want you to know there is always a home to come back to.

There is an Israeli flag outside my house in Kansas. There is a folded note in my wallet. I carry both.
